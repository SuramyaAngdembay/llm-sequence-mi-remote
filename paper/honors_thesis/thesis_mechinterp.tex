\chapter*{ABSTRACT}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{ABSTRACT}

%%%%% CHANGED: abstract rewritten around dimensionality reduction (CTMC chapter removed)
Insider-threat detection requires distinguishing unusual activity from malicious activity.
This thesis studies that distinction through principal component analysis, language-model
surprisal, and sparse representations of a model's internal states on the synthetic CERT
release 4.2 dataset. PCA scores daily activity by its reconstruction error. A language model
trained on benign users scores the same kind of activity by how difficult its textual
description is to predict. Separating predictions about a user's profile from predictions
about their behavior improves the language model's ranking of insiders without retraining.
Sparse-feature analysis then examines where selected internal features activate and how their
removal changes predictions. These features contribute to behavior prediction, but the measured
response does not establish an attack-specific mechanism. The main conclusion is that improving
an anomaly score and explaining the model's internal computation are separate achievements:
excluding profile predictions improves ranking, while profile information can still influence
the behavior predictions that remain.

\chapter{Principal Component Analysis}

\section{Principal Components: Motivation and Formulation}
Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a classical technique for identifying
orthogonal directions along which the data exhibits the greatest variance. In applications such as behavioral cybersecurity logs,
the raw feature space can be high-dimensional and strongly correlated, which complicates
visualization, statistical modeling, and anomaly detection. PCA addresses these issues by
constructing an orthonormal coordinate system whose axes (the \emph{principal components})
are ordered by the amount of variance they explain. This chapter presents the mathematical
formulation of PCA in the general $p$-dimensional setting, the inverse transformation that
reconstructs data from the reduced space, and the reconstruction error that motivates PCA-based
anomaly scoring, followed by low- and high-dimensional illustrations. Historically,
the geometric origins of PCA trace back to Pearson, while the familiar algebraic
eigenvalue formulation was developed by Hotelling \cite{pearson1901,hotelling1933,jolliffe1986}.


PCA represents each observation as a numerical vector in $\mathbb{R}^p$ and approximates the
data with a linear subspace. Its variance criterion summarizes dominant variation; it does not
guarantee that high-variance directions are the most useful ones for detecting anomalies.
Euclidean distance must also be meaningful in the chosen feature space. Because PCA
is sensitive to the relative magnitude of features, the features are standardized first.

Let $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ be a data matrix with $n$ observations and $p$ features, whose
$i$th row is the observation $\mathbf{x}_i^\top$. With the sample mean
$\bar{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{x}_i$ and the vector of ones
$\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^n$, the centered data matrix is
\[
X_c \;=\; X - \mathbf{1}\bar{\mathbf{x}}^\top,
\]
which subtracts the mean from every observation. The sample covariance matrix is
\begin{equation}
S \;=\; \frac{1}{n-1}X_c^\top X_c \in \mathbb{R}^{p\times p},
\label{eq:cov-matrix}
\end{equation}
which is symmetric and positive semidefinite and therefore has an orthonormal eigendecomposition.

PCA looks for the unit direction $\mathbf{a}$ along which the projected scores
$z_i = \mathbf{a}^\top(\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}})$ vary most. Their variance is
$\mathbf{a}^\top S\mathbf{a}$, so the first principal direction solves
\begin{equation}
\max_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^p}\; \mathbf{a}^\top S \mathbf{a} \quad \text{subject to}\quad \mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{a} = 1.
\label{eq:pca-opt}
\end{equation}
With a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$, setting the gradient of
$\mathbf{a}^\top S\mathbf{a} - \lambda(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{a} - 1)$ to zero gives
\begin{equation}
S\mathbf{a} = \lambda \mathbf{a}.
\label{eq:eig}
\end{equation}
The principal directions are therefore eigenvectors of $S$, and each eigenvalue is the variance
explained along its direction \cite{hotelling1933,jolliffe1986}. Ordering the eigenvalues
$\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_p \ge 0$ with orthonormal eigenvectors $\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_p$
gives $S = U\Lambda U^\top$, with $U = [\mathbf{u}_1\;\cdots\;\mathbf{u}_p]$ and
$\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_p)$.

To reduce the dimension to $q < p$, keep the first $q$ directions,
$U_q = [\mathbf{u}_1\;\cdots\;\mathbf{u}_q]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}$. The reduced data, or
\emph{principal component scores}, are
\begin{equation}
Z \;=\; X_c U_q \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times q},
\label{eq:projection}
\end{equation}
whose $i$th row is $\mathbf{z}_i^\top = (U_q^\top \mathbf{x}_{c,i})^\top$. Since the total variance
is $\mathrm{tr}(S) = \sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j$, the proportion of variance explained by the first $q$
components is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PVE}(q) \;=\; \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{q}\lambda_j}{\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_j},
\label{eq:evr}
\end{equation}
and $q$ is commonly chosen so that $\mathrm{PVE}(q)$ exceeds a fixed threshold such as $0.95$.

\section{Inverse PCA and Reconstruction Error}
PCA provides dimensionality reduction, and the \emph{inverse PCA transformation} approximates the
original data from the reduced space:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{X} \;=\; Z\,U_q^\top + \mathbf{1}\bar{\mathbf{x}}^\top,
\qquad
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_i \;=\; U_q\mathbf{z}_i + \bar{\mathbf{x}},
\label{eq:inverse-pca}
\end{equation}
where $Z$ is the reduced data, $U_q$ holds the principal components and $\bar{\mathbf{x}}$ is the
mean vector. For the centered observation $\mathbf{x}_{c,i} = \mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}}$ this
is $\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{c,i} = U_qU_q^\top\mathbf{x}_{c,i}$, the orthogonal projection onto the
principal subspace $\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_q\}$.

The reconstruction error measures the difference between the original and the reconstructed data.
For one observation it is
\begin{equation}
e_i \;=\; \|\mathbf{x}_i - \widehat{\mathbf{x}}_i\|_2^2 \;=\; \|(I - U_qU_q^\top)\,\mathbf{x}_{c,i}\|_2^2 ,
\label{eq:recon-error}
\end{equation}
the squared Euclidean distance from $\mathbf{x}_{c,i}$ to the principal subspace, and over $n$
observations it is the mean squared error
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MSE} \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \widehat{\mathbf{x}}_i\|_2^2 .
\label{eq:mse}
\end{equation}
A model built from normal data reconstructs normal observations well, so a large reconstruction
error may indicate an anomaly. This is the basis of the PCA anomaly score used in this thesis.

\section{Higher Dimensions and Anomaly Scoring}
PCA is often most valuable when $p$ is large and direct visualization is not possible.
To illustrate this, consider a dataset where each observation corresponds to a country and
each feature corresponds to a measured quantity, producing points in $\mathbb{R}^{17}$.
For example, one may treat the weekly per-person consumption of 17 food categories as
a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{17}$. Although we cannot visualize $\mathbb{R}^{17}$ directly, PCA
provides low-dimensional views (e.g., $q=1$ or $q=2$) that reveal dominant contrasts among
countries.

Let $x_k\in\mathbb{R}^{17}$ denote the centered consumption vector for country $k$.
The first PC score is $z_{k1} = u_1^\top x_k$, and the second is $z_{k2}=u_2^\top x_k$.
Countries separated strongly along PC1 exhibit the greatest differences along the dominant
consumption pattern, while PC2 captures the second most important pattern not explained
by PC1.

\section{The CERT Dataset and Insider Cases}
\label{sec:cert}
The CERT Insider Threat Test Dataset is a collection of synthetic datasets produced by the CERT
Division of Carnegie Mellon University's Software Engineering Institute, with ExactData, LLC,
under sponsorship from DARPA \cite{glasser2013,certdataset2016}. Each dataset simulates an
organization in which ordinary employees go about their work and a few simulated insiders act out
a scripted malicious scenario. This thesis uses release 4.2, which simulates 1,000 employees whose
recorded activity runs from 2 January 2010 to 17 May 2011. The data contain activity logs
(logons and logoffs, removable-device connections, file operations, emails and web visits),
organizational records (role, business unit, functional unit, department, team, and whether the
employee is an IT administrator), and five personality scores for every employee.

Every log entry is an event $e = (u, \tau, s, \mathbf{a})$: a user $u \in \mathcal{U}$, a time
$\tau$, a source $s$ (logon, device, file, email or web) and a list of attributes $\mathbf{a}$,
with $|\mathcal{U}| = 1{,}000$. The unit of observation is the \emph{user-day} $D = (u, t)$, one
user on one calendar day, whose events are $E_{u,t} = \{ e : e \text{ is an event of } u \text{ on
day } t\}$. Every method in this thesis maps a user-day to an anomaly score $s(D) \in \mathbb{R}$,
and a larger score means a more unusual day.



\label{sec:insider-case}
The dataset's answer key names each simulated insider, the scenario that insider follows, and the
malicious events involved \cite{certdataset2016}. Release 4.2 contains 70 insiders in three
scenarios (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}).

The language-model experiment uses the official incident window of each insider. If $I_t$ is
the calendar interval of day $t$, and $[a_u,b_u)$ is the answer key's incident interval, then
\begin{equation}
y(u,t)=\mathbb{I}\!\left[I_t\cap[a_u,b_u)\ne\varnothing\right]
\label{eq:day-label}
\end{equation}
for an official insider, and $y(u,t)=0$ for other users. This identifies an
\emph{incident-window day}; it does not label every action on that day as malicious.
An insider's other days are called \emph{window-negative}, because absence from the window
does not verify that all activity was benign. The calendar join uses December 28, 2009 as
the day-index origin. Correct alignment gives 1,362 incident-window days from all 70 insiders
among the 330,295 days with extracted sessions; another 530 calendar-window days have no
session and are outside that observation domain.

The PCA experiment uses a separate day-level feature table and its event-day flag: 966 days
contain recorded malicious events (85, 861 and 20 by scenario). This is a different target
from incident-window membership. Both experiments identify the same 70 official insiders,
but their daily records and inputs differ, so their day-level metrics are not interchangeable.

Incident-window days account for about $0.41\%$ of all session user-days. Calling every day
negative would therefore give high accuracy while detecting no incidents. We instead evaluate
rankings. A threshold on an anomaly score determines a true positive rate, the fraction of
positive cases flagged, and a false positive rate, the fraction of negative cases flagged.
The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve plots these rates as the threshold varies.
Its area (AUC) is the probability that a positive case outranks a negative case, giving half
credit for a tie. User-level AUC compares official insiders with non-insiders after taking
each user's maximum daily score. A value of 1 is perfect ranking and 0.5 is chance-level
ranking. This does not by itself measure which day an incident occurred.

\section{PCA Experiment: Preparation and Results}
\label{sec:pca-data}

The method above needs each observation as a vector of $p$ numerical features, so the
activity of each observation is summarized into one row $\mathbf{x}_i^\top$ of a matrix
$X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$. Here an observation is a user-day, described by the day-level
features of Le et al. \cite{le2020analyzing} (Table~\ref{tab:pca-features}). Release 4.2 gives
$n = 330{,}452$ user-days with 502 features once identifiers and time indices are removed.
Because PCA is sensitive to scale, each column is standardized with the mean and standard
deviation of the training rows. Standardization needs a positive standard deviation, so the 193
columns that never vary among the training days are removed, leaving $p = 309$ features, or 299
without the organizational and personality attributes, the PCA counterpart of the behavior-only
score of \chref{ch:lm}.

PCA is fit on the 287,961 days of the 851 training users of Section~\ref{sec:lm-data}, the users
that also train the language model; all of them are benign, so PCA sees no insider and no
held-out benign user. It keeps the first $q$ principal directions $U_q$, with $q$ the smallest
number whose proportion of variance explained \eqref{eq:evr} reaches $95\%$; this gives $q = 71$
directions, which explain $95.1\%$ of the variance ($q = 63$ without the profile attributes). Each
observation is reduced to its scores and mapped back to the feature space with the inverse PCA
transformation \eqref{eq:inverse-pca}, and its anomaly score is its reconstruction error $e_i$
\eqref{eq:recon-error}; the mean squared error \eqref{eq:mse} summarizes a set of observations.
Benign behavior is reconstructed well by the dominant directions of benign variation, so a large
$e_i$ marks an observation that those directions cannot explain.

The median reconstruction error is 6.6 on the days of the 79 held-out benign users and 17.0 on
the 966 malicious days, and $6.4\%$ of the malicious days exceed the $99$th percentile of the
training errors, against $0.5\%$ of the held-out benign days. Scoring each user by their largest
error gives the user-level AUCs of Table~\ref{tab:pca-results} and the ROC curve of
Figure~\ref{fig:roc}. For all 70 insiders, the user AUC is 0.777. Removing profile attributes changes it little.
The methods use different representations and sets of observed days; this is not a controlled
comparison of model architectures. The last PCA row shows why the constant columns are
removed. They include visits to leak and hacking websites and logons to the supervisor's computer,
which no training day contains and which occur, in the evaluation data, almost only on malicious
days. Left in their raw units, as some software does for a column with zero standard deviation,
they carry $99.7\%$ of the malicious days' squared error.

\section{From Activations to Token Probabilities}
\label{sec:lm-softmax}
An activation is a computed value. For a single unit, the calculation is
\begin{equation}
s=w_1z_1+w_2z_2+b,\qquad h=\phi(s),\qquad
\phi(s)=\max(0,s).
\label{eq:lm-neuron}
\end{equation}
\noindent
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
$z_1,z_2$: input values & $w_1,w_2$: connection weights \\
$b$: bias (added offset) & $s$: weighted sum; $h$: activation \\
$\phi$: ReLU, which clips negative inputs to zero & $\max$: the larger argument
\end{tabular}

\noindent With fixed weights and bias, two inputs give
\[
\begin{array}{c}
(w_1,w_2,b)=(2,-1,1)\\[2pt]
(z_1,z_2)=(1,2)\ \Longrightarrow\ s=1\ \Longrightarrow\ h=1,\\
(z_1,z_2)=(1,4)\ \Longrightarrow\ s=-1\ \Longrightarrow\ h=0.
\end{array}
\]
Thus changing an activation does not require changing a weight.
For a layer with $m$ inputs and $q$ units, the same computation is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf h=\phi(W\mathbf z+\mathbf b),\qquad
W\in\mathbb R^{q\times m},\quad
\mathbf z\in\mathbb R^m,\quad \mathbf b,\mathbf h\in\mathbb R^q.
\label{eq:lm-dense-layer}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbb R^m$ means a column of $m$ real numbers; the matrix dimensions
give rows by columns. Bold letters collect the scalar values above, and
$\phi$ acts on each entry. An MLP composes such layers. The stored weights
and biases form its parameters, denoted $\theta$; its activations depend
on the input. The ReLU calculation is a teaching example.

For the language model, let $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ be a record's $n$ tokens.
Position $t$ has observed token $x_t$ and prefix $x_{<t}=(x_1,\ldots,x_{t-1})$.
The embedding map $E$ assigns each token a vector of width $d$:
\[
\mathbf h_j^{(0)}=E(x_j)\in\mathbb R^d,\qquad j=1,\ldots,n.
\]
The index $j$ locates a token; the superscript $k$ below counts completed
transformer blocks. Let $H^{(k)}$ be the matrix whose row $j$ is
$(\mathbf h_j^{(k)})^\top$, where $\top$ means transpose.
Qwen3-8B uses $L=36$ blocks and width $d=4096$ \cite{qwen3,qwen3config}.
One block computes
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
U^{(k)}
 &=H^{(k-1)}+\mathcal A_k\!\left(\mathcal N_{k,1}(H^{(k-1)})\right),\\
H^{(k)}
 &=U^{(k)}+\mathcal M_k\!\left(\mathcal N_{k,2}(U^{(k)})\right),
 \qquad k=1,\ldots,L.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:lm-block}
\end{equation}
\noindent
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
$H^{(k)},U^{(k)}\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}$ & full-block and intermediate activations \\
$\mathcal A_k$ & causal attention: position $j$ reads positions at most $j$ \\
$\mathcal N_{k,1},\mathcal N_{k,2}$ & row-wise RMS normalization before each branch \\
$\mathcal M_k$ & gated MLP, applied separately at each position
\end{tabular}

The additions retain the incoming state and add each branch's contribution;
these are the residual connections. Qwen's MLP uses a gated activation:
for an input column $\mathbf u\in\mathbb R^d$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal M_k(\mathbf u)
 &=W_{\mathrm{down}}
   \left[\operatorname{SiLU}(W_{\mathrm{gate}}\mathbf u)
                    \odot(W_{\mathrm{up}}\mathbf u)\right],\\
\operatorname{SiLU}(a)&=\frac{a}{1+e^{-a}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:lm-gated-mlp}
\end{equation}
Here $a$ is a scalar, $e$ is the natural exponential base, and $\odot$
multiplies corresponding entries. SiLU acts entry by entry.
The gate and up matrices have dimensions $d_{\mathrm{ff}}\times d$;
the down matrix has dimensions $d\times d_{\mathrm{ff}}$, with
$d_{\mathrm{ff}}=12288$. Their block index is suppressed.
Appendix~\ref{sec:lm-block-details} gives the normalization and causal
attention calculations. The parameters $\theta$ now collect all stored
model weights, including embeddings, attention, MLPs, and output weights.

For each candidate output token $v$ in the vocabulary $\mathcal V$,
the output layer produces a logit:
\begin{equation}
o_{t,v}=\mathbf w_v^\top
              \mathcal N_f(\mathbf h_{t-1}^{(L)}).
\label{eq:lm-logit}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf w_v\in\mathbb R^d$ is the candidate's output-weight vector
and $\mathcal N_f$ is the final normalization. The state at $t-1$
predicts token $t$, so it cannot use that token or later ones.
Softmax converts the logits into probabilities:
\begin{equation}
p_{t,v}
 :=p_\theta(x_t=v\mid x_{<t})
 =\frac{\exp(o_{t,v})}{\sum_{v'\in\mathcal V}\exp(o_{t,v'})}.
\label{eq:softmax}
\end{equation}
The notation $:=$ defines a quantity; $\mid$ means given; $\exp(a)=e^a$.
The sum includes every vocabulary member $v'$, including $v$.
Thus $v$ is a candidate, while $x_t$ is the observation whose probability
we will score.

\section{Surprisal and Benign-Only Adaptation}
\label{sec:lm-train}
The observed-token loss, or \emph{surprisal}, is
\begin{equation}
\ell_t=-\log p_{t,x_t}.
\label{eq:lm-surprisal}
\end{equation}
All logarithms are natural, giving losses in nats. For an illustrative
vocabulary $(\texttt{0},\texttt{3},\texttt{8})$,
\[
(o_{t,v})_v=(0,\log 8,0)
\ \Longrightarrow\ (p_{t,v})_v=(0.1,0.8,0.1)
\ \Longrightarrow\ \ell_t=-\log(0.8)\approx0.223
\quad\text{if }x_t=\texttt{3}.
\]
The subscript $v$ outside parentheses collects the candidates' entries,
and $\approx$ means approximately equal.
A less probable observation, with probability $0.01$, has loss $4.605$.
The logarithm makes successive prediction losses additive:
\begin{equation}
-\log p_\theta(x_2,\ldots,x_n\mid x_1)
 =\sum_{t=2}^{n}-\log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t})
 =\sum_{t=2}^{n}\ell_t.
\label{eq:lm-chain-rule}
\end{equation}
This is the chain rule of probability, conditioning on the first token.
It does not assume token independence. The sum is the negative
log-likelihood of the remaining observed sequence.

We adapt pretrained Qwen3-8B on benign records using LoRA corrections:
\begin{equation}
W'=W+GH,\qquad
G\in\mathbb R^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r},\quad
H\in\mathbb R^{r\times d_{\mathrm{in}}},\qquad r=16.
\label{eq:lm-lora}
\end{equation}
Here $W$ is a selected frozen matrix with $d_{\mathrm{in}}$ inputs
and $d_{\mathrm{out}}$ outputs; $W'$ is its adapted value and
$r$ limits the correction's rank.
Only the factors $G,H$ are trained. The fixed LoRA scale is absorbed
into $G$, and the equation describes evaluation with training dropout
(random masking of adapter inputs) disabled.
The correction's output lies in the span of $G$'s $r$ columns.
This rank bound applies to each matrix, not the full nonlinear network.

Let $\theta_0$ denote the frozen base parameters and $\eta$ collect
the trainable factors, so $\theta=(\theta_0,\eta)$. Adaptation minimizes
\begin{equation}
J(\theta_0,\eta)=
\sum_{D\in\mathcal B}\sum_{t=2}^{n_D}
-\log p_{\theta_0,\eta}\bigl(x_t^{(D)}\mid x_{<t}^{(D)}\bigr).
\label{eq:lm-objective}
\end{equation}
The set $\mathcal B$ contains benign training days; $D$ identifies a
record, $n_D$ its token count, and $(D)$ marks its tokens.
$J$ sums their prediction losses. QLoRA stores the frozen base weights
in 4-bit form to reduce memory use \cite{hu2022lora,dettmers2023qlora}.
The factor count is $r(d_{\mathrm{in}}+d_{\mathrm{out}})$ rather than
$d_{\mathrm{in}}d_{\mathrm{out}}$: 131,072 instead of 16,777,216 for
a 4,096-by-4,096 matrix, or about $0.78\%$.

Benign-only refers to this adaptation, not Qwen's original pretraining.
After adaptation, both $\theta_0$ and $\eta$ stay fixed. Evaluation
measures loss without updating either.

\section{Day Scores and the Profile--Behavior Decomposition}
\label{sec:lm-score}
For one day $D$, define its scored positions and average loss by
\begin{equation}
\mathcal T=\{2,\ldots,n\},\qquad N=|\mathcal T|=n-1,\qquad
\bar\ell(D)=\frac1N\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\ell_t.
\label{eq:lm-day-score}
\end{equation}
Here $n\geq2$ is the record length, $|\mathcal T|$ counts its scored
positions, and the bar denotes an average. The first token is unscored.
For employee $u$ with observed-day set $\mathcal D_u$, the user score is
\begin{equation}
\bar\ell(u)=\max_{D\in\mathcal D_u}\bar\ell(D).
\label{eq:lm-user-score}
\end{equation}
This selects their most unusual day. Longer histories provide more
opportunities for a high maximum; a high score alone does not establish malice.

\label{sec:lm-decomp}
Partition the scored positions into profile ($P$) and behavior ($B$):
\[
\mathcal T=\mathcal T_P\cup\mathcal T_B,\qquad
\mathcal T_P\cap\mathcal T_B=\varnothing.
\]
The union $\cup$ includes both groups; the intersection $\cap$ is empty
($\varnothing$), so no position belongs to both. For class $c\in\{P,B\}$,
\[
N_c=|\mathcal T_c|,\qquad
L_c=\sum_{t\in\mathcal T_c}\ell_t,\qquad
\bar\ell_c=\frac{L_c}{N_c}.
\]
These are its count, total loss, and mean loss. Both classes are nonempty.
Suppressing the day argument, put $N=N_P+N_B$ and $L=L_P+L_B$. Then
\begin{equation}
\bar\ell=\frac{L}{N}
        =\frac{N_P}{N}\bar\ell_P+\frac{N_B}{N}\bar\ell_B,
\label{eq:lm-decomp}
\end{equation}
and removing profile predictions from the score gives
\begin{equation}
\bar\ell_B=\frac{L_B}{N_B}=\frac{L-L_P}{N-N_P}.
\label{eq:lm-behavior}
\end{equation}
Both the loss and token count are removed. The input is unchanged:
behavior predictions can still depend on preceding profile text.
For each score view, user maxima are recalculated using that daily score.

For an illustration, let both days have one profile and four behavior
targets, giving weights 0.2 and 0.8:

Behavior-only scoring raises user AUC from 0.589 to 0.825: insiders

Changing the scored targets leaves all hidden activations unchanged.
An intervention instead edits a chosen layer and recomputes the
downstream predictions. Using the activation matrix $H^{(k)}$
from \eqref{eq:lm-block}, write
\begin{equation}
\widetilde H^{(k)}=\mathcal I(H^{(k)}),\qquad
\Delta\bar\ell_B=
\mathcal S_{B,k}(\widetilde H^{(k)})-\mathcal S_{B,k}(H^{(k)}).
\label{eq:lm-intervention-bridge}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal I$ is the edit, the tilde marks the edited state,
and $\mathcal S_{B,k}$ runs the remaining blocks, output layer,
and behavior-loss calculation with fixed parameters and observed
targets. Thus $\Delta\bar\ell_B$ measures a change in prediction
loss caused by the specified internal edit.

\chref{ch:mech} specifies this edit through selected sparse features
of the adapted-minus-base activations. Its corrected experiment
removes their decoded contribution. Steering and patching are
other intervention policies; Appendix~\ref{sec:lm-intervention-types}
defines them and the stronger requirement of causal abstraction
\cite{geiger2025abstraction}. The current results do not establish
a formal causal abstraction.

The score result concerns one model, representation, and synthetic
benchmark. It does not establish transfer to real organizations,
identify which internal computation uses profile information,
or show that a user's highest-scoring day is their incident day.

\chapter{Interpretable Dimensionality Reduction of the Fine-Tuned Model}
\label{ch:mech}

\section{Overview and Motivation}
\chref{ch:lm} examined the score from the outside: which tokens its surprisal comes
from. \emph{Mechanistic interpretability} asks what the model computes internally
\cite{bricken2023monosemanticity,cunningham2023sparse}. This chapter answers that question with
a form of dimensionality reduction designed for interpretation. PCA (\chref{ch:pca}) reduces
data to a few dense directions, each of which mixes many original features. A \emph{sparse
autoencoder} instead describes each observation with a few directions drawn from a much larger
dictionary; because only a few are active at once, the directions, or \emph{features}, provide candidate patterns to investigate.
Sparsity alone does not guarantee that a feature has one interpretable meaning. We apply it to the change that benign fine-tuning made to the
model's internal representations, and test two things about the features that best separate
malicious from benign days: where they activate, and whether editing them changes the anomaly
score.

\section{Hidden States and the Adaptation Difference}
The model processes text through a stack of 36 layers. After each layer, each token $t$ is
represented by a \emph{hidden state} $\mathbf{h}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ with $d = 4096$. Running
the same text through the model before fine-tuning (the \emph{base} model) and after it (the
\emph{adapted} model) gives two hidden states for every token. Their difference,
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\delta}_t \;=\; \mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{ada}}_t - \mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{base}}_t
\;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{d},
\label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
is the net difference in token $t$'s representation between the two model runs. We study the
hidden states after layer 26.

\section{A Sparse Dictionary and a Worked Example}
Recall the PCA reconstruction of \chref{ch:pca}: a centered observation is projected onto
$q<p$ orthonormal directions and reconstructed by their weighted sum. A sparse autoencoder
(SAE) also minimizes reconstruction error, but uses an overcomplete dictionary of $m>d$
directions and activates at most $k$ directions for each token \cite{gao2024scaling}.

Let $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and the positive coordinate scales $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ be estimated
from the dictionary's fitting data, and put $S=\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})$.
The implementation first standardizes each difference vector and then encodes it:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_t&=S^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\delta}_t-\boldsymbol{\mu}),\\
\mathbf{z}_t&=\mathrm{TopK}_k\!\left(\operatorname{ReLU}
  (W_{\mathrm{enc}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_t+\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{enc}})\right).
\end{split}
\label{eq:sae-encode}
\end{equation}
ReLU sets negative encoder outputs to zero, and TopK retains the $k$ largest entries.
Some of those entries may already be zero, so the code has at most $k$ nonzero values.
The reconstruction in the model's original coordinates is
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_t
 =\boldsymbol{\mu}+S W_{\mathrm{dec}}\mathbf{z}_t
 =\boldsymbol{\mu}+S\sum_f z_{f,t}\mathbf{w}_f,
\label{eq:sae-decode}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{w}_f$ is decoder column $f$. Training minimizes squared reconstruction error
in standardized coordinates. The decoder has no additional learned bias.

Here $d=4096$, $m=8192$ and $k=4$. The corrected dictionary is fitted for 20 epochs on two
million sampled token differences at layer 26. The reconstruction objective has no label
term, but the sampling procedure is label-aware: it preserves the 171,917 token rows from
discovery-positive days and samples the remaining rows from the rest of the evaluation
population. Confirmation-user inputs can therefore enter dictionary fitting, although
their labels do not prioritize that sampling or enter feature ranking. This is a
\emph{transductive} dictionary, not a dictionary fitted on an independent training population.
The low-dimensional object is each token's sparse code; the dictionary as a whole has more
directions than the original space.

For an illustration, take $d = 2$, a dictionary of $m = 3$ unit directions $\mathbf{w}_1 = (1,\;0)$,
$\mathbf{w}_2 = (0,\;1)$ and $\mathbf{w}_3 = (0.6,\;0.8)$, one active feature ($k = 1$),
$\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{enc}}=\mathbf{0}$, $S=I$, and $W_{\mathrm{enc}} = W_{\mathrm{dec}}^\top$. For
$\boldsymbol{\delta} = (1.2,\;1.6)$ the scores are $\mathbf{a} = (1.2,\;1.6,\;2.0)$, so feature 3
is kept and $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}} = 2.0\,\mathbf{w}_3 = (1.2,\;1.6)$ with zero error.
With only the two axes available, the best single direction would leave an error of
$1.2^2 = 1.44$. A different token, $\boldsymbol{\delta}' = (1.0,\;0.1)$, is described by feature 1
with error $0.01$: each token uses the few features that fit it best, which is \emph{sparse
coding}.

\section{Choosing the Features to Test}
The 70 insiders are divided into 35 discovery and 35 confirmation users. Feature selection
uses corrected discovery labels and excludes the confirmation users. For each feature $f$,
the ranking statistic is
\begin{equation}
g_f=\bar z_f^{(+)}-\bar z_f^{(-)},
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
the difference between its mean token activation in the positive and negative discovery
populations. The selected set $\mathcal{F}$ contains the five largest gaps. The active
control set $\mathcal{C}$ uses five other features with gaps near zero and nonzero activation
on at least $0.2\%$ of ranking tokens. This activity threshold does not match either the
activation frequency or the physical size of the selected edit.

The corrected selected IDs are 3843, 3230, 3199, 3975 and 1394. IDs identify columns of this
particular fitted dictionary; they are not named concepts and cannot be compared directly
with IDs from another fit. The confirmation group excludes users from feature ranking,
but those users were inspected earlier in the research and enter unlabelled dictionary
fitting. It is therefore a check across ranking groups, not an independent confirmation set.

\section{Where Features Activate}
For a feature $f$ and a token class $c$, the share of the feature's total activation that
lands on class-$c$ tokens is
\begin{equation}
\rho_f(c) \;=\; \frac{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}_c} z_{f,t}}{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} z_{f,t}},
\label{eq:attribution}
\end{equation}
and its \emph{enrichment} compares that share with the class's share of the tokens,
\[
E_f(c) \;=\; \frac{\rho_f(c)}{N_c / N}.
\]
When the denominator is positive, an enrichment of 1 means activation mass is proportional
to token frequency; it does not measure how often the feature fires. Larger values mean
more activation mass per token on class $c$, up to the maximum $1/(N_c/N)$ reached when all
of a feature's activation lands on that class.

\section{Causal Test: Removing Features and Re-Scoring}
Attribution is descriptive. To test whether the selected representation contributes to
prediction, remove its decoded contribution from the adapted hidden state:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf h'_t=\mathbf h_t^{\mathrm{ada}}
 -\alpha S\sum_{f\in\mathcal F}z_{f,t}\mathbf w_f,
\qquad 0\leq\alpha\leq1.
\label{eq:ablation}
\end{equation}
This leaves the original hidden state and its reconstruction residual intact except for the
specified subtraction. At $\alpha=0$ it is an exact no-op; at $\alpha=1$ it removes the
selected decoded contribution. Tokens with no selected activation remain unchanged.
Re-encoding the edited state need not reproduce the intended sparse code, so the experiment
is defined by this physical subtraction, not by assuming independent feature coordinates.

The remaining model layers predict the same target tokens. For a day $D$, let
$\Delta_{\mathcal F}(D)=\bar\ell'_B(D)-\bar\ell_B(D)$ be the change in behavior-target loss.
A positive value means prediction became worse. Two controls answer different questions:
removal of the active-control features tests a separate feature-selection policy, while a
random dictionary direction at the same edited positions is matched to the realized
per-token edit norm within $5\%$. Matching is checked after numerical rounding in the
model's precision. The fixed feasibility policy for those dictionary controls is retained
from the corrected recovery protocol; it is not a new, independently preregistered test.
The active-feature control is not dose matched.

The primary removal strength is fixed at $\alpha=1$. For each incident-window receiver,
the experiment also uses a same-user window-negative day, chosen by nearest calendar day,
preferring the same weekday and using fixed hash tie-breaking. Negative days can be reused.
For a receiver pair $(D^+,D^-)$, the relative window effect is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\tau(D^+,D^-)
={}&[\Delta_{\mathcal F}(D^+)-\Delta_{\mathcal C}(D^+)]\\
 &-[\Delta_{\mathcal F}(D^-)-\Delta_{\mathcal C}(D^-)].
\end{split}
\label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
A positive value would indicate a larger selected-versus-active response inside the incident
window. Pairing is observational: even a positive value would not by itself prove an
attack-specific circuit. Days are averaged within each user, then users are weighted equally;
zero edits and repeated negative-day appearances retain their prescribed weights.

\section{Limitations and Conclusions}
The analysis uses one adapted model and one synthetic benchmark release. Model-seed
variation and transfer to real organizational data remain untested here. The layer and
dictionary settings were informed by earlier experiments; the corrected dictionary is
transductive, its sample prioritizes discovery-positive rows, and its confirmation users were
previously inspected. Feature ranking excludes
those users, but that separation does not make the entire analysis independent.

The controls have distinct scopes. Active-feature removal compares selection policies with
different natural edit sizes. Dictionary directions match edited positions and realized
norms, but do not enumerate every possible alternative intervention. Same-user negative
days control some user differences but are not verified benign ground truth. The reported
intervals are conditional on the fixed model, dictionary, feature sets, pairs and controls.

Finally, an activation at a behavior-token position need not represent behavior alone.
Interpretable labels for individual features require further evidence, and these joint edits
do not identify a complete computational circuit. The corrected ablation is the causal test
reported in this thesis; the earlier donor-search result is not carried forward as current
evidence after label correction.

\chapter{Supporting Mathematics for Language-Model Scoring}
\label{app:lm-math}
This appendix supplies the derivations supporting \chref{ch:lm}. The main
chapter develops the prediction task and its scoring rule; the arguments
below explain why the logarithm is useful, how it relates to the output
scores, and how the evaluation averages are constructed. All logarithms
here are natural logarithms.

\section{Why Additive Surprise Has a Logarithmic Form}
\label{sec:why-log}
Let $p$ be an event's probability, with $0<p\leq1$, and let $S(p)$ be
the amount of surprise assigned to it. Require that $S$ strictly decreases
as $p$ increases: rarer events are more surprising. Let $q$ be the
probability of a second, independent event, with $0<q\leq1$.
Independence makes the probability of both events $pq$. Requiring their
surprises to add gives
\begin{equation}
S(pq)=S(p)+S(q).
\label{eq:surprise-additive}
\end{equation}
This is a requirement on the surprise measure for independent events;
it does not assert that successive text tokens are independent.

To find its form, introduce nonnegative real numbers $x=-\ln p$ and
$y=-\ln q$, so $p=e^{-x}$ and $q=e^{-y}$. Here $e$ is the base of the
natural exponential. Define a new function $g(x)=S(e^{-x})$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:surprise-additive} becomes
\[
g(x+y)=g(x)+g(y).
\]
The function $g$ is increasing because $e^{-x}$ decreases with $x$
and $S$ decreases with probability. This additive relation is Cauchy's
functional equation. Its increasing solutions on nonnegative real
numbers have the form $g(x)=cx$, where $c$ is a positive constant.
Substitution therefore gives
\begin{equation}
S(p)=-c\ln p,\qquad c>0.
\label{eq:surprise-log}
\end{equation}
The constant $c$ sets the measurement unit. Choosing $c=1$ gives
surprisal in nats, as used in \chref{ch:lm} \cite{shannon1948}.
A certain event, with $p=1$, has zero surprise. As $p$ approaches zero,
surprise grows without bound. Every tenfold decrease in probability
adds $\ln 10$ nats. Applying the logarithm to the chain of conditional
token probabilities gives \eqref{eq:lm-chain-rule} without requiring
independent tokens.

\section{Surprisal Written in Terms of Logits}
Fix one prediction position $t$, where $x_t$ is the observed token
and $x_{<t}$ is the preceding sequence. Let $\theta$ denote the fixed
model parameters and $\mathcal V$ its vocabulary. Its size is
$V=|\mathcal V|$, where the bars count members of the set. For each
candidate token $v$ in the vocabulary, let $o_v$ be its output logit,
and let $\mathbf o$ collect all those logits. Define the
\emph{log-sum-exp} function, abbreviated $\mathrm{LSE}$, by taking
the logarithm of the sum of exponentiated logits. Substituting
softmax into the observed token's surprisal $\ell_t$ gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\ell_t
 &=-\log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t})
   =\mathrm{LSE}(\mathbf o)-o_{x_t},\\
\mathrm{LSE}(\mathbf o)
 &=\log\sum_{v\in\mathcal V}e^{o_v}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:lse-surprisal}
\end{equation}
The subscript $x_t$ selects the logit of the observed token.
The summation adds over all vocabulary members, and $e$ is the
natural exponential base.

Let $o_{\max}=\max_{v\in\mathcal V}o_v$ be the largest logit.
The sum of exponentials includes at least one term $e^{o_{\max}}$,
and none of its $V$ terms is larger than that term. Thus
\[
e^{o_{\max}}\leq\sum_{v\in\mathcal V}e^{o_v}
             \leq V e^{o_{\max}}.
\]
Taking logarithms, which preserve these inequalities, gives
\[
o_{\max}\leq\mathrm{LSE}(\mathbf o)
          \leq o_{\max}+\log V.
\]
For two candidate tokens $a$ and $b$, the common softmax
denominator cancels in their probability ratio:
\[
\frac{p_\theta(x_t=a\mid x_{<t})}
     {p_\theta(x_t=b\mid x_{<t})}=e^{o_a-o_b}.
\]
Only logit differences determine the probabilities. Having the
largest logit alone does not guarantee a small surprisal: many
other candidates may have similar logits and share the probability.

\section{Maximum Likelihood in a Frequency Model}
\label{sec:lm-mle-proof}
Consider a model that assigns a fixed probability to each of
$K$ token types, regardless of preceding text. Let $j$ index
those types from 1 to $K$, let $N_j$ count occurrences of type
$j$ in the training observations, and let
$N=\sum_{j=1}^{K}N_j$ be the total count. Write $p_j$ for the
probability assigned to type $j$ and $\mathbf p=(p_1,\ldots,p_K)$
for their collection. For the interior calculation, suppose each
$N_j$ is positive and each $p_j$ is positive.

The summed negative log-likelihood is
$-\sum_{j=1}^{K}N_j\log p_j$, subject to the probabilities adding
to one. Introduce a real number $\lambda$, the Lagrange multiplier,
which enforces this constraint. Define the Lagrangian
$\mathcal L(\mathbf p,\lambda)$ to be the objective plus
$\lambda$ times the constraint residual:
\[
\mathcal L(\mathbf p,\lambda)
 =-\sum_{j=1}^{K}N_j\log p_j
  +\lambda\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_j-1\right).
\]
The parenthesized expression is zero exactly when the probabilities
sum to one. Differentiating with respect to each $p_j$ while holding
the other quantities fixed, and setting the derivative to zero,
gives
\[
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial p_j}
 =-\frac{N_j}{p_j}+\lambda=0,
\qquad p_j=\frac{N_j}{\lambda}.
\]
The symbol $\partial$ denotes this partial derivative. Summing
the last equality over token types and imposing a total probability
of one gives $\lambda=N$. Hence the minimizing probabilities are
\begin{equation}
\widehat p_j=\frac{N_j}{N},\qquad j=1,\ldots,K.
\label{eq:lm-mle}
\end{equation}
The hat marks a fitted value. The objective is convex: along a
straight segment between two positive probability vectors,
its value lies at or below the line joining the endpoint values.
Thus this stationary point gives the minimum under the
constraint. If additional types have zero
observed count, the optimum in the unrestricted frequency model
assigns them zero probability. This frequency calculation
illustrates the objective; a contextual language model with
shared parameters and finite softmax logits is a different
model and need not reproduce these exact frequencies.

\section{Why Subtracting Class Means Gives a Different Score}
\label{sec:lm-naive-proof}
For one day, let $N_P$ and $N_B$ count scored profile and
behavior tokens, and let $N=N_P+N_B$ be their total. Assume
both classes are nonempty. Let $\bar\ell_P$ and $\bar\ell_B$
be their average surprisals, and let $\bar\ell$ be the
average across all scored tokens. From \eqref{eq:lm-decomp},
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\bar\ell-\bar\ell_P
 &=\frac{N_P}{N}\bar\ell_P+\frac{N_B}{N}\bar\ell_B-\bar\ell_P\\
 &=\frac{N_B}{N}\bigl(\bar\ell_B-\bar\ell_P\bigr).
\end{split}
\label{eq:lm-naive}
\end{equation}
The result is a scaled difference between class means, not
the behavior mean. For a numerical illustration, take two days,
named C and D, each with behavior-token share $N_B/N=0.8$.
Give Day C profile mean 3.0 and behavior mean 1.2, and Day D
profile mean 0.2 and behavior mean 0.8. Subtracting means gives
$0.8(1.2-3.0)=-1.44$ for C and $0.8(0.8-0.2)=0.48$ for D.
It ranks D above C even though C's behavior has higher surprisal.
Removing a token class from an average requires removing both
its summed loss and its token count, as in \eqref{eq:lm-behavior}.

\section{User AUC and Equal-Insider Fold Averages}
\label{sec:lm-auc-proof}
Fix one scoring rule. Let $M$ be the number of insider users
and $n_-$ the number of benign comparison users, both positive.
The minus subscript labels the negative class; it does not
mean subtraction. Let $m$ index insiders from 1 to $M$ and
$j$ index benign users from 1 to $n_-$. Write $s_m^+$ for
insider $m$'s maximum daily score and $s_j^-$ for benign
user $j$'s maximum daily score. The superscripts $+$ and $-$
label positive and negative classes, not powers.

Define the indicator $\mathbb I[\text{condition}]$ to be
1 when the enclosed condition is true and 0 otherwise.
The user AUC for the fold containing insider $m$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AUC}_m=\frac{1}{n_-}\sum_{j=1}^{n_-}
\left(\mathbb I[s_m^+>s_j^-]
 +\tfrac12\mathbb I[s_m^+=s_j^-]\right).
\label{eq:auc}
\end{equation}
Each comparison contributes 1 if the insider outranks the
benign user, one half for a tie, and 0 otherwise. Let
$a_{mj}$ name that contribution for pair $(m,j)$.
The overall equal-insider mean, denoted $\mathrm{AUC}$,
therefore satisfies
\[
\mathrm{AUC}
 =\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathrm{AUC}_m
 =\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}
     \frac{1}{n_-}\sum_{j=1}^{n_-}a_{mj}
 =\frac{1}{Mn_-}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{n_-}a_{mj}.
\]
Every insider is compared with the same benign users,
so this is also the pooled user AUC over all $Mn_-$ pairs.
That identity does not establish an analogous equality for
day-level fold averages or AP, whose comparison populations
and weights differ.

For illustration, let four benign users have full scores
$(0.93,0.70,0.85,0.60)$ and one insider have score $0.77$.
The insider outranks two benign users, giving AUC $2/4=0.50$.
If the behavior-only scores of those users are
$(0.41,0.55,0.70,0.48)$ and the insider's is $0.93$,
the insider outranks all four, giving AUC $4/4=1.00$.
These examples explain the calculation and are not reported
experimental observations.

\section{Normalization and Causal Attention}
\label{sec:lm-block-details}
For a vector $\mathbf z\in\mathbb R^m$, RMS normalization is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal N(\mathbf z)=
\frac{\boldsymbol\gamma\odot\mathbf z}
{\sqrt{\epsilon+\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}z_i^2}}.
\label{eq:lm-rmsnorm}
\end{equation}
Here $m$ is the vector length, $i$ its coordinate index,
$\boldsymbol\gamma\in\mathbb R^m$ a learned scale,
$\epsilon>0$ a fixed stabilizing constant, and $\odot$ entrywise
multiplication. Each normalization in \eqref{eq:lm-block} has its
own scale parameters and operates on each token row separately.

For one attention head, let $\mathbf q_j$, $\mathbf k_s$, and
$\mathbf v_s$ be its query at position $j$, key at position $s$,
and value at position $s$, each with $d_h$ coordinates.
The attention weights $a_{js}$ and the head output $\mathbf y_j$ are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
a_{js}
 &=\frac{\exp(\mathbf q_j^\top\mathbf k_s/\sqrt{d_h})}
         {\sum_{u=1}^{j}\exp(\mathbf q_j^\top\mathbf k_u/\sqrt{d_h})},
      &&1\leq s\leq j,\\
a_{js}&=0, &&s>j,\\
\mathbf y_j&=\sum_{s=1}^{j}a_{js}\mathbf v_s.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:lm-attention}
\end{equation}
The index $u$ runs over permitted key positions in the denominator;
$\top$ is transpose, and $\sqrt{d_h}$ scales the dot products.
The zero weights prevent position $j$ from reading future positions.
Concatenating the head outputs and applying a learned output matrix
gives the attention branch contribution $\mathcal A_k$.

In Qwen, queries and keys in this formula are the projected vectors
after query/key normalization and position-dependent rotary transformations.
Multiple query heads share key/value projections through grouped-query
attention \cite{qwen3}. These operations are included in
$\mathcal A_k$; the weighted sum is its causal mixing step.
The MLP then transforms each position separately, while residual
addition preserves a path for the incoming activation.

input limit. This is within-day likelihood scoring, not next-day
forecasting or generation of replacement activity.

The historical conversion program omitted four hyphenated fields:
\path{file_n-to_usb1}, \path{file_n-from_usb1},
\path{file_n-file_act3}, and \path{file_n-disk1}.
If a missing field appears as zero, that is a conversion default,
not a measured absence of activity. The findings apply to this
recorded representation.

\section{Intervention Policies and Causal Abstraction}
\label{sec:lm-intervention-types}
The same downstream computation can be tested with different
edits. At a fixed layer and token position, let
$\mathbf h\in\mathbb R^d$ be the original activation and
$\mathbf h'$ its replacement. Common choices are
\begin{equation}
\begin{array}{ll}
\text{direction steering:}
 &\mathbf h'=\mathbf h+\alpha\mathbf v,\\[2pt]
\text{coordinate patching:}
 &\mathbf h'_J=\mathbf h^{\mathrm{src}}_J,\quad
  \mathbf h'_{\bar J}=\mathbf h_{\bar J},\\[2pt]
\text{feature removal:}
 &\mathbf h'=\mathbf h-\alpha\mathbf c_{\mathcal F}.
\end{array}
\label{eq:lm-intervention-policies}
\end{equation}
\noindent
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
$d$ & activation width \\
$\alpha$ & chosen scalar intervention strength \\
$\mathbf v\in\mathbb R^d$ & chosen steering direction \\
$J,\bar J$ & selected coordinates and their complement \\
$\mathbf h^{\mathrm{src}}$ & donor state at the same layer and an aligned position \\
$\mathbf c_{\mathcal F}\in\mathbb R^d$ & decoded contribution of selected feature set $\mathcal F$
\end{tabular}

The corrected experiment uses the final policy, with the physical
contribution defined by \eqref{eq:ablation}. A joint change in prediction
loss supports a role under that edit; it does not by itself assign a
semantic meaning to each feature.

A causal abstraction requires an additional model of the proposed
high-level computation and a correspondence between interventions
\cite{geiger2025abstraction}. For deterministic models with well-defined
outcomes, the exact agreement condition can be written
\begin{equation}
\tau\!\left(M_{\mathrm{low}}(\xi;\iota)\right)
 =M_{\mathrm{high}}\!\left(\tau_C(\xi);\omega(\iota)\right).
\label{eq:lm-abstraction}
\end{equation}
\noindent
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
$\xi$ & input or background context \\
$\iota$ & an allowed low-level intervention \\
$M_{\mathrm{low}}(\xi;\iota)$ & low-level variable assignment under that intervention \\
$M_{\mathrm{high}}$ & proposed high-level causal model \\
$\tau$ & map from low-level to high-level variable assignments \\
$\tau_C$ & corresponding map of background contexts \\
$\omega$ & map from low-level to high-level interventions
\end{tabular}

The equality must hold over the specified admissible contexts
and interventions for exact abstraction; finite experiments can
assess agreement on the cases tested. A sparse representation
alone does not supply the high-level model or these maps.
The thesis's corrected feature-removal result is not a demonstration
of \eqref{eq:lm-abstraction}.
