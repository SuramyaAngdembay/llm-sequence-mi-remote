\chapter*{ABSTRACT}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{ABSTRACT}

%%%%% CHANGED: abstract rewritten around dimensionality reduction (CTMC chapter removed)
Insider-threat detection requires distinguishing unusual activity from malicious activity.
This thesis studies that distinction through principal component analysis, language-model
surprisal, and sparse representations of a model's internal states on the synthetic CERT
release 4.2 dataset. PCA scores daily activity by its reconstruction error. A language model
trained on benign users scores the same kind of activity by how difficult its textual
description is to predict. Separating predictions about a user's profile from predictions
about their behavior improves the language model's ranking of insiders without retraining.
Sparse-feature analysis then examines where selected internal features activate and how their
removal changes predictions. These features contribute to behavior prediction, but the measured
response does not establish an attack-specific mechanism. The main conclusion is that improving
an anomaly score and explaining the model's internal computation are separate achievements:
excluding profile predictions improves ranking, while profile information can still influence
the behavior predictions that remain.

\chapter{Principal Component Analysis}

\section{Principal Components: Motivation and Formulation}
Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a classical technique for identifying
orthogonal directions along which the data exhibits the greatest variance. In applications such as behavioral cybersecurity logs,
the raw feature space can be high-dimensional and strongly correlated, which complicates
visualization, statistical modeling, and anomaly detection. PCA addresses these issues by
constructing an orthonormal coordinate system whose axes (the \emph{principal components})
are ordered by the amount of variance they explain. This chapter presents the mathematical
formulation of PCA in the general $p$-dimensional setting, the inverse transformation that
reconstructs data from the reduced space, and the reconstruction error that motivates PCA-based
anomaly scoring, followed by low- and high-dimensional illustrations. Historically,
the geometric origins of PCA trace back to Pearson, while the familiar algebraic
eigenvalue formulation was developed by Hotelling \cite{pearson1901,hotelling1933,jolliffe1986}.


PCA represents each observation as a numerical vector in $\mathbb{R}^p$ and approximates the
data with a linear subspace. Its variance criterion summarizes dominant variation; it does not
guarantee that high-variance directions are the most useful ones for detecting anomalies.
Euclidean distance must also be meaningful in the chosen feature space. Because PCA
is sensitive to the relative magnitude of features, the features are standardized first.

Let $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ be a data matrix with $n$ observations and $p$ features, whose
$i$th row is the observation $\mathbf{x}_i^\top$. With the sample mean
$\bar{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{x}_i$ and the vector of ones
$\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^n$, the centered data matrix is
\[
X_c \;=\; X - \mathbf{1}\bar{\mathbf{x}}^\top,
\]
which subtracts the mean from every observation. The sample covariance matrix is
\begin{equation}
S \;=\; \frac{1}{n-1}X_c^\top X_c \in \mathbb{R}^{p\times p},
\label{eq:cov-matrix}
\end{equation}
which is symmetric and positive semidefinite and therefore has an orthonormal eigendecomposition.

PCA looks for the unit direction $\mathbf{a}$ along which the projected scores
$z_i = \mathbf{a}^\top(\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}})$ vary most. Their variance is
$\mathbf{a}^\top S\mathbf{a}$, so the first principal direction solves
\begin{equation}
\max_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^p}\; \mathbf{a}^\top S \mathbf{a} \quad \text{subject to}\quad \mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{a} = 1.
\label{eq:pca-opt}
\end{equation}
With a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$, setting the gradient of
$\mathbf{a}^\top S\mathbf{a} - \lambda(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{a} - 1)$ to zero gives
\begin{equation}
S\mathbf{a} = \lambda \mathbf{a}.
\label{eq:eig}
\end{equation}
The principal directions are therefore eigenvectors of $S$, and each eigenvalue is the variance
explained along its direction \cite{hotelling1933,jolliffe1986}. Ordering the eigenvalues
$\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_p \ge 0$ with orthonormal eigenvectors $\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_p$
gives $S = U\Lambda U^\top$, with $U = [\mathbf{u}_1\;\cdots\;\mathbf{u}_p]$ and
$\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_p)$.

To reduce the dimension to $q < p$, keep the first $q$ directions,
$U_q = [\mathbf{u}_1\;\cdots\;\mathbf{u}_q]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}$. The reduced data, or
\emph{principal component scores}, are
\begin{equation}
Z \;=\; X_c U_q \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times q},
\label{eq:projection}
\end{equation}
whose $i$th row is $\mathbf{z}_i^\top = (U_q^\top \mathbf{x}_{c,i})^\top$. Since the total variance
is $\mathrm{tr}(S) = \sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j$, the proportion of variance explained by the first $q$
components is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PVE}(q) \;=\; \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{q}\lambda_j}{\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_j},
\label{eq:evr}
\end{equation}
and $q$ is commonly chosen so that $\mathrm{PVE}(q)$ exceeds a fixed threshold such as $0.95$.

\section{Inverse PCA and Reconstruction Error}
PCA provides dimensionality reduction, and the \emph{inverse PCA transformation} approximates the
original data from the reduced space:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{X} \;=\; Z\,U_q^\top + \mathbf{1}\bar{\mathbf{x}}^\top,
\qquad
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_i \;=\; U_q\mathbf{z}_i + \bar{\mathbf{x}},
\label{eq:inverse-pca}
\end{equation}
where $Z$ is the reduced data, $U_q$ holds the principal components and $\bar{\mathbf{x}}$ is the
mean vector. For the centered observation $\mathbf{x}_{c,i} = \mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}}$ this
is $\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{c,i} = U_qU_q^\top\mathbf{x}_{c,i}$, the orthogonal projection onto the
principal subspace $\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_q\}$.

The reconstruction error measures the difference between the original and the reconstructed data.
For one observation it is
\begin{equation}
e_i \;=\; \|\mathbf{x}_i - \widehat{\mathbf{x}}_i\|_2^2 \;=\; \|(I - U_qU_q^\top)\,\mathbf{x}_{c,i}\|_2^2 ,
\label{eq:recon-error}
\end{equation}
the squared Euclidean distance from $\mathbf{x}_{c,i}$ to the principal subspace, and over $n$
observations it is the mean squared error
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MSE} \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \widehat{\mathbf{x}}_i\|_2^2 .
\label{eq:mse}
\end{equation}
A model built from normal data reconstructs normal observations well, so a large reconstruction
error may indicate an anomaly. This is the basis of the PCA anomaly score used in this thesis.

\section{Higher Dimensions and Anomaly Scoring}
PCA is often most valuable when $p$ is large and direct visualization is not possible.
To illustrate this, consider a dataset where each observation corresponds to a country and
each feature corresponds to a measured quantity, producing points in $\mathbb{R}^{17}$.
For example, one may treat the weekly per-person consumption of 17 food categories as
a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{17}$. Although we cannot visualize $\mathbb{R}^{17}$ directly, PCA
provides low-dimensional views (e.g., $q=1$ or $q=2$) that reveal dominant contrasts among
countries.

Let $x_k\in\mathbb{R}^{17}$ denote the centered consumption vector for country $k$.
The first PC score is $z_{k1} = u_1^\top x_k$, and the second is $z_{k2}=u_2^\top x_k$.
Countries separated strongly along PC1 exhibit the greatest differences along the dominant
consumption pattern, while PC2 captures the second most important pattern not explained
by PC1.

\section{The CERT Dataset and Insider Cases}
\label{sec:cert}
The CERT Insider Threat Test Dataset is a collection of synthetic datasets produced by the CERT
Division of Carnegie Mellon University's Software Engineering Institute, with ExactData, LLC,
under sponsorship from DARPA \cite{glasser2013,certdataset2016}. Each dataset simulates an
organization in which ordinary employees go about their work and a few simulated insiders act out
a scripted malicious scenario. This thesis uses release 4.2, which simulates 1,000 employees whose
recorded activity runs from 2 January 2010 to 17 May 2011. The data contain activity logs
(logons and logoffs, removable-device connections, file operations, emails and web visits),
organizational records (role, business unit, functional unit, department, team, and whether the
employee is an IT administrator), and five personality scores for every employee.

Every log entry is an event $e = (u, \tau, s, \mathbf{a})$: a user $u \in \mathcal{U}$, a time
$\tau$, a source $s$ (logon, device, file, email or web) and a list of attributes $\mathbf{a}$,
with $|\mathcal{U}| = 1{,}000$. The unit of observation is the \emph{user-day} $D = (u, t)$, one
user on one calendar day, whose events are $E_{u,t} = \{ e : e \text{ is an event of } u \text{ on
day } t\}$. Every method in this thesis maps a user-day to an anomaly score $s(D) \in \mathbb{R}$,
and a larger score means a more unusual day.



\label{sec:insider-case}
The dataset's answer key names each simulated insider, the scenario that insider follows, and the
malicious events involved \cite{certdataset2016}. Release 4.2 contains 70 insiders in three
scenarios (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}).

The language-model experiment uses the official incident window of each insider. If $I_t$ is
the calendar interval of day $t$, and $[a_u,b_u)$ is the answer key's incident interval, then
\begin{equation}
y(u,t)=\mathbb{I}\!\left[I_t\cap[a_u,b_u)\ne\varnothing\right]
\label{eq:day-label}
\end{equation}
for an official insider, and $y(u,t)=0$ for other users. This identifies an
\emph{incident-window day}; it does not label every action on that day as malicious.
An insider's other days are called \emph{window-negative}, because absence from the window
does not verify that all activity was benign. The calendar join uses December 28, 2009 as
the day-index origin. Correct alignment gives 1,362 incident-window days from all 70 insiders
among the 330,295 days with extracted sessions; another 530 calendar-window days have no
session and are outside that observation domain.

The PCA experiment uses a separate day-level feature table and its event-day flag: 966 days
contain recorded malicious events (85, 861 and 20 by scenario). This is a different target
from incident-window membership. Both experiments identify the same 70 official insiders,
but their daily records and inputs differ, so their day-level metrics are not interchangeable.

Incident-window days account for about $0.41\%$ of all session user-days. Calling every day
negative would therefore give high accuracy while detecting no incidents. We instead evaluate
rankings. A threshold on an anomaly score determines a true positive rate, the fraction of
positive cases flagged, and a false positive rate, the fraction of negative cases flagged.
The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve plots these rates as the threshold varies.
Its area (AUC) is the probability that a positive case outranks a negative case, giving half
credit for a tie. User-level AUC compares official insiders with non-insiders after taking
each user's maximum daily score. A value of 1 is perfect ranking and 0.5 is chance-level
ranking. This does not by itself measure which day an incident occurred.

\section{PCA Experiment: Preparation and Results}
\label{sec:pca-data}

The method above needs each observation as a vector of $p$ numerical features, so the
activity of each observation is summarized into one row $\mathbf{x}_i^\top$ of a matrix
$X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$. Here an observation is a user-day, described by the day-level
features of Le et al. \cite{le2020analyzing} (Table~\ref{tab:pca-features}). Release 4.2 gives
$n = 330{,}452$ user-days with 502 features once identifiers and time indices are removed.
Because PCA is sensitive to scale, each column is standardized with the mean and standard
deviation of the training rows. Standardization needs a positive standard deviation, so the 193
columns that never vary among the training days are removed, leaving $p = 309$ features, or 299
without the organizational and personality attributes, the PCA counterpart of the behavior-only
score of \chref{ch:lm}.

PCA is fit on the 287,961 days of the 851 training users of Section~\ref{sec:lm-data}, the users
that also train the language model; all of them are benign, so PCA sees no insider and no
held-out benign user. It keeps the first $q$ principal directions $U_q$, with $q$ the smallest
number whose proportion of variance explained \eqref{eq:evr} reaches $95\%$; this gives $q = 71$
directions, which explain $95.1\%$ of the variance ($q = 63$ without the profile attributes). Each
observation is reduced to its scores and mapped back to the feature space with the inverse PCA
transformation \eqref{eq:inverse-pca}, and its anomaly score is its reconstruction error $e_i$
\eqref{eq:recon-error}; the mean squared error \eqref{eq:mse} summarizes a set of observations.
Benign behavior is reconstructed well by the dominant directions of benign variation, so a large
$e_i$ marks an observation that those directions cannot explain.

The median reconstruction error is 6.6 on the days of the 79 held-out benign users and 17.0 on
the 966 malicious days, and $6.4\%$ of the malicious days exceed the $99$th percentile of the
training errors, against $0.5\%$ of the held-out benign days. Scoring each user by their largest
error gives the user-level AUCs of Table~\ref{tab:pca-results} and the ROC curve of
Figure~\ref{fig:roc}. For all 70 insiders, the user AUC is 0.777. Removing profile attributes changes it little.
The methods use different representations and sets of observed days; this is not a controlled
comparison of model architectures. The last PCA row shows why the constant columns are
removed. They include visits to leak and hacking websites and logons to the supervisor's computer,
which no training day contains and which occur, in the evaluation data, almost only on malicious
days. Left in their raw units, as some software does for a column with zero standard deviation,
they carry $99.7\%$ of the malicious days' squared error.

\section{From Reconstruction to Prediction}
\chref{ch:pca} scored an observation by the reconstruction error of a low-dimensional linear
model. This chapter scores a user-day, one user's activity on one day, by how difficult its
text record is for a large language model, fine-tuned (further trained) only on benign activity,
to predict. Both scores ask the same question: how badly
does a model of normal behavior account for this observation? Two ideas from \chref{ch:pca}
carry over. The fine-tuning changes each adapted weight matrix only by a low-rank correction (Section~\ref{sec:lm-train}),
and \chref{ch:mech} decomposes the model's internal change with a sparse relative of PCA.
Scoring activity by how poorly a model trained on normal activity predicts it follows earlier
work on log and insider-threat data \cite{du2017deeplog,tuor2017deep}.

The new contribution of this chapter is to take the score apart. We show that it splits
exactly into a part describing \emph{who} the user is and a part describing \emph{what} the
user did, and examine how counting those predictions changes the ranking of insiders.

The approach starts with a \emph{pretrained} model that has already learned to predict text.
We adapt it to records from benign employees, then keep its parameters fixed during evaluation.
Each day's record is broken into small text pieces called \emph{tokens}. Given the actual
preceding tokens, the model assigns a probability to the next observed token. Low probabilities
give larger scores, which we average over the day. This measures how unusual the record is
under the model; it is not a probability that the employee is malicious. Finally, we compare
the score for all tokens with a score that counts only predictions about behavior, while
leaving the input unchanged.

\section{Data Preparation and Text Representation}
\label{sec:lm-data}
We use CERT release 4.2, a synthetic dataset of employee activity containing ordinary work
and scripted insider incidents. The language model reads activity grouped into \emph{sessions}, each running from a logon to the
matching logoff on one computer, with features following the extraction of Le et al.
\cite{le2020analyzing}: time of day, duration, computer, and counts of logon, device, file, email
and web events. We write these measurements as text so that the language model can read them.
For example, a session lasting 489 minutes with 3 emails is represented by fields such as
\texttt{duration=489} and \texttt{n\_email=3}. The model receives these activity summaries,
rather than the employees' email messages.

Each user-day $D$ is written as the text
\[

\]
lines joined by newlines: the organizational attributes (\texttt{DAY}), the personality scores
(\texttt{PSY}), the number of sessions (\texttt{SESSIONS}) and one line of \texttt{key=value}
features per session; $\Vert$ denotes joining the lines. Days have between one and nine
sessions (two thirds have one), and the longest texts have about 1,300 tokens, within the

\section{Next-Token Probability and Surprisal}
\label{sec:lm-softmax}
A token that the model assigns probability $0.8$ is less surprising than one assigned
probability $0.01$. We measure this difference with \emph{surprisal}, the negative natural
logarithm of the assigned probability: these examples give approximately $0.223$ and $4.605$.
Lower probabilities therefore contribute more to the anomaly score.

We score the text that was actually observed. At position $t$, the model uses only the preceding
tokens $x_{<t}=(x_1,\ldots,x_{t-1})$ to predict $x_t$. The evaluator knows the whole record,
but the model's prediction at that position cannot use the target token or later tokens.
This evaluates a recorded day's predictability; it does not generate a new activity record or
forecast the next day.

The probabilities come from the model's internal representation of that preceding text.
Its 36 processing layers produce a \emph{context vector}
$\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{4096}$, a list of numbers summarizing the text for the next
prediction \cite{vaswani2017attention}. Each possible token $v$ in the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$
has a learned vector $\mathbf{w}_v$. Their dot product gives a score, or \emph{logit},
$o_v=\mathbf{w}_v^\top\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$; collecting these scores gives
$\mathbf{o}=W_{\mathrm{out}}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$. The \emph{softmax} converts the scores into
probabilities by exponentiating each score and dividing by the sum over all possible tokens:
\begin{equation}
p_\theta(x_t = v \mid x_{<t}) \;=\; \frac{e^{o_v}}{\sum_{v' \in \mathcal{V}} e^{o_{v'}}} ,
\label{eq:softmax}
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ denotes the model's learned parameters. For finite logits, these probabilities
are positive and sum to 1. Only differences between logits matter: for two tokens $a$ and $b$,
$p(a)/p(b)=e^{o_a-o_b}$.

The \emph{surprisal} of the actual token is
\begin{equation}
\ell_t \;=\; -\log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})
\;=\; \mathrm{LSE}(\mathbf{o}) \;-\; o_{x_t},
\qquad \mathrm{LSE}(\mathbf{o}) = \log\sum_{v} e^{o_v},
\label{eq:lse-surprisal}
\end{equation}
where the log-sum-exp satisfies $o_{\max} \le \mathrm{LSE}(\mathbf{o}) \le o_{\max} + \log|\mathcal{V}|$.
The log-sum-exp term combines the scores of all possible tokens into one value. Subtracting
the observed token's logit gives its surprisal. Thus the measure depends on the probability
assigned to the observed token, including how strongly other tokens compete with it.

%%%%% MOVED here from the removed CTMC chapter (why -log p measures surprise)

\label{sec:why-log}
Why use the negative logarithm? Besides assigning larger values to rarer events, it turns a
product of probabilities into a sum of scores. This lets us add the surprises of successive
predictions. More formally, two requirements on a measure of surprise $S(p)$ leave
only the logarithm, the measure of information introduced by Shannon \cite{shannon1948}. First,
rarer events are more surprising, so $S$ decreases in $p$. Second, the surprises of independent
events add, and since $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A)P(B)$ this means
\begin{equation}
S(pq) \;=\; S(p) + S(q) \qquad \text{for all } p, q \in (0, 1].
\label{eq:surprise-additive}
\end{equation}
Writing $p = e^{-x}$ and $g(x) = S(e^{-x})$ turns \eqref{eq:surprise-additive} into
$g(x+y)=g(x)+g(y)$: adding two inputs adds their outputs. This is Cauchy's functional
equation, whose only increasing solutions are $g(x)=c\,x$. Hence
\begin{equation}
S(p) \;=\; -c \ln p, \qquad c > 0,
\label{eq:surprise-log}
\end{equation}
where $c$ only fixes the unit; using the natural logarithm with $c=1$ measures surprise in
\emph{nats}. A certain event carries
no surprise, an event thought impossible would be infinitely surprising, and every tenfold drop in
probability adds the same $\ln 10$ nats.

For a single observed event, the surprisal is also its \emph{negative log-likelihood}. By the
chain rule of probability, the probability of a day's text is the product of each token's
probability given the tokens before it, so
\begin{equation}
-\log p_\theta(x_2, \dots, x_n \mid x_1)
\;=\; \sum_{t=2}^{n} -\log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})
\;=\; \sum_{t=2}^{n} \ell_t :
\label{eq:lm-chain-rule}
\end{equation}
the total surprisal of a day is the negative log-likelihood of its text. This chain rule uses
conditional probabilities and does not assume that successive tokens are independent.
Because days differ in
length, the anomaly score of Section~\ref{sec:lm-score} divides this
total by the number of scored tokens.

\section{Learning From Benign Days Only}
\label{sec:lm-train}
Our starting model is Qwen3-8B \cite{qwen3}, a language model already trained on a large text
corpus. \emph{Fine-tuning}, also called adaptation, is further training that makes it better
at predicting our activity records. Only this further training is restricted to benign
employees. Once adaptation finishes, we freeze the model: scoring evaluation records does
not update its parameters.

During adaptation, the model predicts each training token from its preceding text. The
training objective adds up the resulting surprisal, or prediction \emph{loss}. Reducing this
loss encourages the model to assign higher probabilities to the benign records it sees.
Formally, we seek parameters that minimize
\begin{equation}
\theta^\star \;=\; \arg\min_{\theta}\; \sum_{D \in \mathcal{B}} \;\sum_{t=2}^{n_D}\;
-\log p_\theta\bigl(x^{(D)}_t \mid x^{(D)}_{<t}\bigr),
\label{eq:lm-objective}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{B}$ is the set of benign training days and $x^{(D)}$ is the text of day $D$,
with $n_D$ tokens. This is \emph{maximum-likelihood estimation}: choosing parameters that
make the observed training text more probable. Iterative training seeks a low-loss solution;
the notation does not claim that we find a global minimum.

A simple frequency model illustrates the idea. Suppose it assigns one probability $p_j$
to each of $K$ tokens, without considering preceding text. If token $j$ occurs $N_j$ times
among $N$ training tokens, minimizing $\sum_j N_j(-\log p_j)$ subject to $\sum_j p_j=1$
with a Lagrange multiplier gives
\begin{equation}
\hat{p}_j \;=\; \frac{N_j}{N},
\label{eq:lm-mle}
\end{equation}
the observed token frequencies. A token appearing in 80 of 100 observations receives
probability $0.8$ in this simple model. The language model instead learns probabilities that
depend on the preceding text. It reduces its loss by \emph{gradient descent}, repeatedly
adjusting trainable parameters in a direction that lowers training loss. This motivates
using surprisal to detect unusual records, but rarity alone does not establish malicious intent.

We restrict which parameters can change using \emph{low-rank adaptation} (LoRA)
\cite{hu2022lora}. The original weight matrices stay fixed, and selected matrices receive a
trainable correction. For a square matrix, this has the form $W'=W+GH$, with
$G\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$, $H\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}$, and $r=16$.
The two smaller matrices provide the correction while using far fewer trainable
parameters.\footnote{For a $4096\times4096$ matrix, the two rank-16 factors contain 131,072
parameters, about $0.78\%$ of the 16,777,216 entries in the original matrix. Rectangular
weight matrices use the corresponding input and output dimensions.}
For any input $\mathbf{x}$, the change $GH\mathbf{x}$ lies in the span of the 16 columns of
$G$. This gives a limited connection to PCA: each adapted matrix changes its output along
at most 16 directions. The composed nonlinear network's hidden-state difference need not
lie in a 16-dimensional subspace.

The quantized version, QLoRA \cite{dettmers2023qlora}, stores the fixed base weights in a
4-bit representation to reduce memory use, while learning the low-rank corrections.
Our adaptation makes one pass over the 287,827 benign user-days from the 851 training users
in Table~\ref{tab:splits}. No insider records enter this training objective.

\section{Anomaly Scores and Their Decomposition}
\label{sec:lm-score}
The previous sections explain how the model assigns a score to each predicted token.
To compare whole days, we average those scores. Averaging prevents a longer record from
receiving a larger score simply because it contains more terms in the sum.
Let $\mathcal{T}$ be the set of scored positions of a day (every token except the first, which
has nothing before it) and $N = |\mathcal{T}|$. The anomaly score of the day is its average
token surprisal,
\begin{equation}
\bar{\ell} \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}} \ell_t .
\label{eq:lm-day-score}
\end{equation}
A high $\bar{\ell}$ means that the model adapted on benign days found this day hard to
predict. A user's score is the score of their most unusual day,
\[
\bar{\ell}(u) \;=\; \max_{D \in \mathcal{D}_u} \bar{\ell}(D),
\]
where $\mathcal{D}_u$ is the set of that user's observed days. This maximum asks whether the
user has any particularly unusual day. It is a chosen summary rule, so users with longer
observed histories have more opportunities to receive a high score.


\label{sec:lm-decomp}
The full average includes predictions about both the user's profile and their activity.
A rare profile can therefore raise the score even when the activity is ordinary. We examine
this contribution by adding the losses in each token class separately.
Divide the scored positions into profile and behavior positions,
$\mathcal{T} = \mathcal{T}_P \cup \mathcal{T}_B$, with $N = N_P + N_B$.
Let $L_P=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_P}\ell_t$ and
$L_B=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_B}\ell_t$ be their summed losses, and let $L=L_P+L_B$.
For nonempty classes, the class means are $\bar{\ell}_P=L_P/N_P$ and
$\bar{\ell}_B=L_B/N_B$. Then
\begin{equation}
\bar{\ell}
\;=\; \frac{1}{N}\bigl(L_P + L_B\bigr)
\;=\; \frac{N_P}{N}\,\bar{\ell}_P \;+\; \frac{N_B}{N}\,\bar{\ell}_B :
\label{eq:lm-decomp}
\end{equation}
the day's score is exactly a weighted average of the profile mean and the behavior mean, weighted
by their shares of the tokens. The behavior-only score
\begin{equation}
\bar{\ell}_B \;=\; \frac{L_B}{N_B} \;=\; \frac{L - L_P}{N - N_P}
\label{eq:lm-behavior}
\end{equation}
uses the same model with no retraining. It adds only behavior-target losses and divides by
the number of those targets. Profile text still precedes the activity text, so it can still
influence the predictions. A \emph{target} here is the token being predicted.

To remove a class from an average, both its summed loss and its token count must be removed.
Simply subtracting the profile mean gives a different quantity:
\begin{equation}
\bar{\ell} - \bar{\ell}_P \;=\; \frac{N_B}{N}\bigl(\bar{\ell}_B - \bar{\ell}_P\bigr) :
\label{eq:lm-naive}
\end{equation}
this measures how much harder the behavior was to predict than the profile, not how hard the behavior
was to predict, and it can reverse the order of two days.


For a numerical illustration, two user-days each have five scored tokens, one profile token and four behavior tokens, so
$N_P/N = 0.2$. Day A belongs to a malicious user with a common profile and unusual behavior; Day B
to a benign user with a rare profile and routine behavior.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Class means and scores of the two example days.}
\label{tab:lm-example}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
day & $\bar{\ell}_P$ & $\bar{\ell}_B$ & full score $\bar{\ell} = 0.2\,\bar{\ell}_P + 0.8\,\bar{\ell}_B$ & behavior-only score \\
\midrule
A (malicious) & 0.1054 & 0.9324 & 0.7670 & 0.9324 \\
B (benign) & 2.9957 & 0.4125 & 0.9292 & 0.4125 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent The full score ranks the benign Day B above the malicious Day A: its profile part favors
Day B by $0.578$ and outweighs a behavior part that favors Day A by $0.416$. The behavior-only
score restores the correct order. The naive subtraction can fail as well: for a Day C with
$\bar{\ell}_P = 3.0$, $\bar{\ell}_B = 1.2$ and a Day D with $\bar{\ell}_P = 0.2$,
$\bar{\ell}_B = 0.8$, equation \eqref{eq:lm-naive} gives $0.8(1.2 - 3.0) = -1.44$ and
$0.8(0.8 - 0.2) = 0.48$, ranking D above C although C's behavior is less predictable.

\section{Evaluation and Corrected Results}
\label{sec:design}

\label{sec:lm-auc} %%%%% NEW label (2026-09-28), cited from Chapter III
Evaluation asks whether larger scores belong to the cases we want to find. There are two
questions: do insider \emph{users} rank above benign users, and do incident-window \emph{days}
rank above negative days? The first uses each user's maximum daily score; the second uses
individual day scores. Success on one does not guarantee success on the other.

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve records the fraction of positives found
and the fraction of negatives flagged as a score threshold varies. Its area, ROC-AUC or
simply AUC, also has a direct ranking interpretation: the share of positive--negative pairs
in which the positive has the larger score, with half credit for ties. A value of 1 means
perfect ranking; 0.5 is chance-level ranking. A value below 0.5 means that the observed
ranking favors negatives more often than positives. Establishing whether a small departure
from 0.5 is reliable requires a separate uncertainty assessment.

With one malicious user $m$ compared against $n_-$ benign users $u_1,\dots,u_{n_-}$, the
area under the ROC curve for $m$ is the fraction of benign users that $m$ outranks,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AUC}_m \;=\; \frac{1}{n_-}\sum_{j=1}^{n_-}\left(\mathbb{I}[\bar{\ell}(m)>\bar{\ell}(u_j)]
+\tfrac12\mathbb{I}[\bar{\ell}(m)=\bar{\ell}(u_j)]\right),
\label{eq:auc}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{I}[\cdot]$ is 1 when the condition holds and 0 otherwise. Averaging over $M$ malicious users gives $\mathrm{AUC} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_m
\mathrm{AUC}_m$. Because every malicious user is compared with the same benign users, this
average equals the pooled AUC over all $M \times n_-$ malicious--benign pairs:
\[
\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}\frac{1}{n_-}\sum_{j} w_{mj}
\;=\; \frac{1}{M\,n_-}\sum_{m}\sum_{j} w_{mj},
\]
where $w_{mj}$ is 1, $\tfrac12$, or 0 for a win, tie, or loss.

For example, suppose four benign users have full scores $(0.93,\;0.70,\;0.85,\;0.60)$ and a
malicious user has full score $0.77$. The malicious user outranks $0.70$ and $0.60$, so
$\mathrm{AUC}_m = 2/4 = 0.50$. If the behavior-only scores are $(0.41,\;0.55,\;0.70,\;0.48)$
for the benign users and $0.93$ for the malicious user, it outranks all four, and
$\mathrm{AUC}_m = 4/4 = 1.00$.


\label{sec:lm-finding}
For the day-level evaluation, we repeat the comparison once for each insider. Each comparison,
called a \emph{fold}, contains all observed days of that insider and all days of the same
79 held-out benign users. Incident-window days are positive; the insider's window-negative
days and all benign-user days are negative. We calculate each fold's metrics and average
them equally across the 70 insiders. The model stays fixed across folds; we do not train
a different model for each comparison. Unlike the user-AUC identity above, averaging
day-metric folds need not equal pooling all days into one calculation.

We also report \emph{average precision} (AP). At a given threshold, \emph{precision} is the
fraction of flagged cases that are positive, while \emph{recall} is the fraction of all
positive cases that are flagged. Lowering the threshold flags more cases and changes both
fractions. AP summarizes precision over the increases in recall; it is not the precision
at one chosen alert threshold. Table~\ref{tab:lm-views} compares the full, behavior-only and
profile-only scores from the same frozen model and input.

\begin{table}[!htbp]
\centering

\label{tab:lm-views}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Scored targets & day AUC & day AP & user AUC \\
\midrule

The user AUC rises from 0.589 to 0.825 when we count only behavior targets. This means that,
with half credit for ties, an insider ranks above a benign user in about $82.5\%$ of the
evaluated user pairs, compared with $58.9\%$ for the full score. These are ranking results,
not percentages of users correctly classified at an alert threshold.

To describe uncertainty in the change, we use a \emph{user bootstrap}: repeatedly sample
the 70 insiders with replacement and recalculate the mean gain, keeping each insider's
full-score and behavior-only AUCs paired. Counting only behavior targets raises user AUC by $0.236$, with a descriptive
95\% user-bootstrap interval $[0.185,0.289]$. This resampling keeps the model and benign
comparison cohort fixed; it does not measure variation across independently trained models.
The improvement comes from changing which prediction losses enter the score.
The profile-only point estimate
is near chance and slightly below it; that alone does not establish a systematic reversed
ranking. The paired score comparison is the direct evidence for the benefit of behavior-only
scoring in this experiment.

AP depends on how common positives are in the comparison population. That fraction is called
\emph{prevalence}. Each fold includes only one insider alongside all 79 benign users, so its
positive days are especially rare: mean fold prevalence is about $0.0714\%$. Pooling all
42,468 days instead includes every insider together and gives $3.21\%$ positive days.
The reported AP values average the individual folds and must be interpreted using that
comparison, rather than the pooled fraction.

This result separates score composition from internal information use. Profile predictions
can dilute the ranking even though the behavior predictions may depend on the preceding
profile text. Excluding those profile predictions from an average does not remove that context.
The result applies to this model, text representation and benchmark, whose evaluation users
were repeatedly examined during development. Better user ranking also does not establish
that the highest-scoring day is the day of the incident. The main conclusion is that
changing the composition of an anomaly score can improve ranking without changing the
model. Explaining how the model uses the information in its input requires a different test;
the next chapter investigates that question by editing internal features.

\chapter{Interpretable Dimensionality Reduction of the Fine-Tuned Model}
\label{ch:mech}

\section{Overview and Motivation}
\chref{ch:lm} examined the score from the outside: which tokens its surprisal comes
from. \emph{Mechanistic interpretability} asks what the model computes internally
\cite{bricken2023monosemanticity,cunningham2023sparse}. This chapter answers that question with
a form of dimensionality reduction designed for interpretation. PCA (\chref{ch:pca}) reduces
data to a few dense directions, each of which mixes many original features. A \emph{sparse
autoencoder} instead describes each observation with a few directions drawn from a much larger
dictionary; because only a few are active at once, the directions, or \emph{features}, provide candidate patterns to investigate.
Sparsity alone does not guarantee that a feature has one interpretable meaning. We apply it to the change that benign fine-tuning made to the
model's internal representations, and test two things about the features that best separate
malicious from benign days: where they activate, and whether editing them changes the anomaly
score.

\section{Hidden States and the Adaptation Difference}
The model processes text through a stack of 36 layers. After each layer, each token $t$ is
represented by a \emph{hidden state} $\mathbf{h}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ with $d = 4096$. Running
the same text through the model before fine-tuning (the \emph{base} model) and after it (the
\emph{adapted} model) gives two hidden states for every token. Their difference,
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\delta}_t \;=\; \mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{ada}}_t - \mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{base}}_t
\;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{d},
\label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
is the net difference in token $t$'s representation between the two model runs. We study the
hidden states after layer 26.

\section{A Sparse Dictionary and a Worked Example}
Recall the PCA reconstruction of \chref{ch:pca}: a centered observation is projected onto
$q<p$ orthonormal directions and reconstructed by their weighted sum. A sparse autoencoder
(SAE) also minimizes reconstruction error, but uses an overcomplete dictionary of $m>d$
directions and activates at most $k$ directions for each token \cite{gao2024scaling}.

Let $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and the positive coordinate scales $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ be estimated
from the dictionary's fitting data, and put $S=\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})$.
The implementation first standardizes each difference vector and then encodes it:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_t&=S^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\delta}_t-\boldsymbol{\mu}),\\
\mathbf{z}_t&=\mathrm{TopK}_k\!\left(\operatorname{ReLU}
  (W_{\mathrm{enc}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_t+\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{enc}})\right).
\end{split}
\label{eq:sae-encode}
\end{equation}
ReLU sets negative encoder outputs to zero, and TopK retains the $k$ largest entries.
Some of those entries may already be zero, so the code has at most $k$ nonzero values.
The reconstruction in the model's original coordinates is
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_t
 =\boldsymbol{\mu}+S W_{\mathrm{dec}}\mathbf{z}_t
 =\boldsymbol{\mu}+S\sum_f z_{f,t}\mathbf{w}_f,
\label{eq:sae-decode}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{w}_f$ is decoder column $f$. Training minimizes squared reconstruction error
in standardized coordinates. The decoder has no additional learned bias.

Here $d=4096$, $m=8192$ and $k=4$. The corrected dictionary is fitted for 20 epochs on two
million sampled token differences at layer 26. The reconstruction objective has no label
term, but the sampling procedure is label-aware: it preserves the 171,917 token rows from
discovery-positive days and samples the remaining rows from the rest of the evaluation
population. Confirmation-user inputs can therefore enter dictionary fitting, although
their labels do not prioritize that sampling or enter feature ranking. This is a
\emph{transductive} dictionary, not a dictionary fitted on an independent training population.
The low-dimensional object is each token's sparse code; the dictionary as a whole has more
directions than the original space.

For an illustration, take $d = 2$, a dictionary of $m = 3$ unit directions $\mathbf{w}_1 = (1,\;0)$,
$\mathbf{w}_2 = (0,\;1)$ and $\mathbf{w}_3 = (0.6,\;0.8)$, one active feature ($k = 1$),
$\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{enc}}=\mathbf{0}$, $S=I$, and $W_{\mathrm{enc}} = W_{\mathrm{dec}}^\top$. For
$\boldsymbol{\delta} = (1.2,\;1.6)$ the scores are $\mathbf{a} = (1.2,\;1.6,\;2.0)$, so feature 3
is kept and $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}} = 2.0\,\mathbf{w}_3 = (1.2,\;1.6)$ with zero error.
With only the two axes available, the best single direction would leave an error of
$1.2^2 = 1.44$. A different token, $\boldsymbol{\delta}' = (1.0,\;0.1)$, is described by feature 1
with error $0.01$: each token uses the few features that fit it best, which is \emph{sparse
coding}.

\section{Choosing the Features to Test}
The 70 insiders are divided into 35 discovery and 35 confirmation users. Feature selection
uses corrected discovery labels and excludes the confirmation users. For each feature $f$,
the ranking statistic is
\begin{equation}
g_f=\bar z_f^{(+)}-\bar z_f^{(-)},
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
the difference between its mean token activation in the positive and negative discovery
populations. The selected set $\mathcal{F}$ contains the five largest gaps. The active
control set $\mathcal{C}$ uses five other features with gaps near zero and nonzero activation
on at least $0.2\%$ of ranking tokens. This activity threshold does not match either the
activation frequency or the physical size of the selected edit.

The corrected selected IDs are 3843, 3230, 3199, 3975 and 1394. IDs identify columns of this
particular fitted dictionary; they are not named concepts and cannot be compared directly
with IDs from another fit. The confirmation group excludes users from feature ranking,
but those users were inspected earlier in the research and enter unlabelled dictionary
fitting. It is therefore a check across ranking groups, not an independent confirmation set.

\section{Where Features Activate}
For a feature $f$ and a token class $c$, the share of the feature's total activation that
lands on class-$c$ tokens is
\begin{equation}
\rho_f(c) \;=\; \frac{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}_c} z_{f,t}}{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} z_{f,t}},
\label{eq:attribution}
\end{equation}
and its \emph{enrichment} compares that share with the class's share of the tokens,
\[
E_f(c) \;=\; \frac{\rho_f(c)}{N_c / N}.
\]
When the denominator is positive, an enrichment of 1 means activation mass is proportional
to token frequency; it does not measure how often the feature fires. Larger values mean
more activation mass per token on class $c$, up to the maximum $1/(N_c/N)$ reached when all
of a feature's activation lands on that class.


If a feature has zero total activation in a group, its location fraction is undefined.
Table~\ref{tab:attribution} reports session-token mass and the number of confirmation users
on whose incident-window days the feature activates at least once.

\section{Causal Test: Removing Features and Re-Scoring}
Attribution is descriptive. To test whether the selected representation contributes to
prediction, remove its decoded contribution from the adapted hidden state:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf h'_t=\mathbf h_t^{\mathrm{ada}}
 -\alpha S\sum_{f\in\mathcal F}z_{f,t}\mathbf w_f,
\qquad 0\leq\alpha\leq1.
\label{eq:ablation}
\end{equation}
This leaves the original hidden state and its reconstruction residual intact except for the
specified subtraction. At $\alpha=0$ it is an exact no-op; at $\alpha=1$ it removes the
selected decoded contribution. Tokens with no selected activation remain unchanged.
Re-encoding the edited state need not reproduce the intended sparse code, so the experiment
is defined by this physical subtraction, not by assuming independent feature coordinates.

The remaining model layers predict the same target tokens. For a day $D$, let
$\Delta_{\mathcal F}(D)=\bar\ell'_B(D)-\bar\ell_B(D)$ be the change in behavior-target loss.
A positive value means prediction became worse. Two controls answer different questions:
removal of the active-control features tests a separate feature-selection policy, while a
random dictionary direction at the same edited positions is matched to the realized
per-token edit norm within $5\%$. Matching is checked after numerical rounding in the
model's precision. The fixed feasibility policy for those dictionary controls is retained
from the corrected recovery protocol; it is not a new, independently preregistered test.
The active-feature control is not dose matched.

The primary removal strength is fixed at $\alpha=1$. For each incident-window receiver,
the experiment also uses a same-user window-negative day, chosen by nearest calendar day,
preferring the same weekday and using fixed hash tie-breaking. Negative days can be reused.
For a receiver pair $(D^+,D^-)$, the relative window effect is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\tau(D^+,D^-)
={}&[\Delta_{\mathcal F}(D^+)-\Delta_{\mathcal C}(D^+)]\\
 &-[\Delta_{\mathcal F}(D^-)-\Delta_{\mathcal C}(D^-)].
\end{split}
\label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
A positive value would indicate a larger selected-versus-active response inside the incident
window. Pairing is observational: even a positive value would not by itself prove an
attack-specific circuit. Days are averaged within each user, then users are weighted equally;
zero edits and repeated negative-day appearances retain their prescribed weights.

\section{Corrected Mechanistic Results}
\label{sec:mech-finding}
The corrected run covers all 1,362 incident-window days and 380 unique matched window-negative
days, giving 1,742 receivers and 13,936 scored rows across eight conditions. The primary
summary uses the 667 incident-window days of the 35 confirmation users. Zero-edit and
implementation checks passed, and the aggregate estimates were independently reproduced.

Removing the selected contribution raises behavior loss relative to baseline and to both
specified controls. This supports a contribution to prediction under this edit. However,
the relative response is smaller on incident-window days than on their matched
window-negative days: the final contrast is negative. The results therefore do not support
an enhanced attack-specific response under this policy.

An intervention's effect on prediction loss is also different from an improvement in anomaly
detection. These estimates do not demonstrate better ranking, semantic identification of the
features, or mediation of the profile's influence. Together with \chref{ch:lm}, they support
two bounded conclusions: score composition affects insider ranking, and the selected sparse
directions contribute to behavior prediction. The second does not explain the first by itself.

\section{Limitations and Conclusions}
The analysis uses one adapted model and one synthetic benchmark release. Model-seed
variation and transfer to real organizational data remain untested here. The layer and
dictionary settings were informed by earlier experiments; the corrected dictionary is
transductive, its sample prioritizes discovery-positive rows, and its confirmation users were
previously inspected. Feature ranking excludes
those users, but that separation does not make the entire analysis independent.

The controls have distinct scopes. Active-feature removal compares selection policies with
different natural edit sizes. Dictionary directions match edited positions and realized
norms, but do not enumerate every possible alternative intervention. Same-user negative
days control some user differences but are not verified benign ground truth. The reported
intervals are conditional on the fixed model, dictionary, feature sets, pairs and controls.

Finally, an activation at a behavior-token position need not represent behavior alone.
Interpretable labels for individual features require further evidence, and these joint edits
do not identify a complete computational circuit. The corrected ablation is the causal test
reported in this thesis; the earlier donor-search result is not carried forward as current
evidence after label correction.
