\chapter*{ABSTRACT}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{ABSTRACT}

%%%%% CHANGED: abstract rewritten around dimensionality reduction (CTMC chapter removed)
Insider threat represents a major cybersecurity challenge to companies, organizations, and government agencies. Detecting malicious insiders is difficult due to massive data volumes, extreme class imbalance, and the subtle nature of behavioral shifts over time. This thesis approaches the problem through dimensionality reduction: describing high-dimensional behavior with a small number of informative directions, and treating what a model of normal behavior fails to explain as a sign of anomaly. First, a classical approach uses Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to reduce behavioral features to their dominant directions of variance, reconstructs each observation from those directions with the inverse PCA transformation, and scores it by its reconstruction error. Second, a large language model is fine-tuned only on benign user-days written as text, with the fine-tuning restricted to low-rank changes of the model's weights, and it scores each day by its average surprisal, the negative log-probability of each token given the tokens before it. We show that this score splits exactly into a profile part, covering organizational and personality attributes, and a behavior part, and that the profile part ranks users backwards: on CERT release 4.2, profile tokens alone place malicious users below benign ones (user-level AUC 0.47), while scoring behavior tokens alone raises user-level AUC from 0.65 to 0.86. Third, we apply an interpretable form of dimensionality reduction to the model itself. A sparse autoencoder, an overcomplete and sparse relative of PCA, is learned on the change that fine-tuning made to the model's internal representations. Four of the five features most associated with malicious days activate almost only on behavioral tokens. On malicious users held out from feature selection, the best of many searched edits that move these features toward a benign colleague's values, rather than another malicious user's, lowers the anomaly score more than the same comparison for control features, by a small margin; averaged over colleagues instead of taking the best, the sign reverses. Splitting the same edits by token class shows that, at full strength, they raise the loss on behavior tokens more than control edits do, while their effect on profile tokens is uncertain. This is evidence about a joint edit of the selected features. Where a feature activates, what it represents and which predictions its edit changes are separate questions. All experiments use the CMU CERT insider threat dataset.

\section{Overview and Motivation}
Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a classical technique for identifying
orthogonal directions along which the data exhibits the greatest variance. In applications such as behavioral cybersecurity logs,
the raw feature space can be high-dimensional and strongly correlated, which complicates
visualization, statistical modeling, and anomaly detection. PCA addresses these issues by
constructing an orthonormal coordinate system whose axes (the \emph{principal components})
are ordered by the amount of variance they explain. This chapter presents the mathematical
formulation of PCA in the general $p$-dimensional setting, the inverse transformation that
reconstructs data from the reduced space, and the reconstruction error that motivates PCA-based
anomaly scoring, followed by low- and high-dimensional illustrations. Historically,
the geometric origins of PCA trace back to Pearson, while the familiar algebraic
eigenvalue formulation was developed by Hotelling \cite{pearson1901,hotelling1933,jolliffe1986}.

\section{PCA in the $p$-Dimensional Setting}
PCA assumes that each observation is a numerical vector in $\mathbb{R}^p$, that the structure of
the data can be approximated by a linear subspace, that directions of larger variance carry more
meaningful variation, and that Euclidean distance is meaningful in the feature space. Because PCA
is sensitive to the relative magnitude of features, the features are standardized first.

Let $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ be a data matrix with $n$ observations and $p$ features, whose
$i$th row is the observation $\mathbf{x}_i^\top$. With the sample mean
$\bar{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{x}_i$ and the vector of ones
$\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^n$, the centered data matrix is
\[
X_c \;=\; X - \mathbf{1}\bar{\mathbf{x}}^\top,
\]
which subtracts the mean from every observation. The sample covariance matrix is
\begin{equation}
S \;=\; \frac{1}{n-1}X_c^\top X_c \in \mathbb{R}^{p\times p},
\label{eq:cov-matrix}
\end{equation}
which is symmetric and positive semidefinite and therefore has an orthonormal eigendecomposition.

PCA looks for the unit direction $\mathbf{a}$ along which the projected scores
$z_i = \mathbf{a}^\top(\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}})$ vary most. Their variance is
$\mathbf{a}^\top S\mathbf{a}$, so the first principal direction solves
\begin{equation}
\max_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^p}\; \mathbf{a}^\top S \mathbf{a} \quad \text{subject to}\quad \mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{a} = 1.
\label{eq:pca-opt}
\end{equation}
With a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$, setting the gradient of
$\mathbf{a}^\top S\mathbf{a} - \lambda(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{a} - 1)$ to zero gives
\begin{equation}
S\mathbf{a} = \lambda \mathbf{a}.
\label{eq:eig}
\end{equation}
The principal directions are therefore eigenvectors of $S$, and each eigenvalue is the variance
explained along its direction \cite{hotelling1933,jolliffe1986}. Ordering the eigenvalues
$\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_p \ge 0$ with orthonormal eigenvectors $\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_p$
gives $S = U\Lambda U^\top$, with $U = [\mathbf{u}_1\;\cdots\;\mathbf{u}_p]$ and
$\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_p)$.

To reduce the dimension to $q < p$, keep the first $q$ directions,
$U_q = [\mathbf{u}_1\;\cdots\;\mathbf{u}_q]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}$. The reduced data, or
\emph{principal component scores}, are
\begin{equation}
Z \;=\; X_c U_q \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times q},
\label{eq:projection}
\end{equation}
whose $i$th row is $\mathbf{z}_i^\top = (U_q^\top \mathbf{x}_{c,i})^\top$. Since the total variance
is $\mathrm{tr}(S) = \sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j$, the proportion of variance explained by the first $q$
components is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PVE}(q) \;=\; \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{q}\lambda_j}{\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_j},
\label{eq:evr}
\end{equation}
and $q$ is commonly chosen so that $\mathrm{PVE}(q)$ exceeds a fixed threshold such as $0.95$.

\section{Inverse PCA and Reconstruction Error}
PCA provides dimensionality reduction, and the \emph{inverse PCA transformation} approximates the
original data from the reduced space:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{X} \;=\; Z\,U_q^\top + \mathbf{1}\bar{\mathbf{x}}^\top,
\qquad
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_i \;=\; U_q\mathbf{z}_i + \bar{\mathbf{x}},
\label{eq:inverse-pca}
\end{equation}
where $Z$ is the reduced data, $U_q$ holds the principal components and $\bar{\mathbf{x}}$ is the
mean vector. For the centered observation $\mathbf{x}_{c,i} = \mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}}$ this
is $\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{c,i} = U_qU_q^\top\mathbf{x}_{c,i}$, the orthogonal projection onto the
principal subspace $\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_q\}$.

The reconstruction error measures the difference between the original and the reconstructed data.
For one observation it is
\begin{equation}
e_i \;=\; \|\mathbf{x}_i - \widehat{\mathbf{x}}_i\|_2^2 \;=\; \|(I - U_qU_q^\top)\,\mathbf{x}_{c,i}\|_2^2 ,
\label{eq:recon-error}
\end{equation}
the squared Euclidean distance from $\mathbf{x}_{c,i}$ to the principal subspace, and over $n$
observations it is the mean squared error
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MSE} \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \widehat{\mathbf{x}}_i\|_2^2 .
\label{eq:mse}
\end{equation}
A model built from normal data reconstructs normal observations well, so a large reconstruction
error may indicate an anomaly. This is the basis of the PCA anomaly score used in this thesis.

\section{Low-Dimensional Geometric Intuition}
In $\mathbb{R}^2$, PCA identifies a new pair of orthonormal axes $(u_1,u_2)$ obtained by rotating
the original coordinate system so that $u_1$ aligns with the direction of maximum spread. If the
data are approximately distributed along a line, reducing from $2$D to $1$D projects the points
onto the line spanned by $u_1$ and discards the orthogonal component (Figure~\ref{fig:pca-2d}).
In $\mathbb{R}^3$, the three directions $(u_1,u_2,u_3)$ are ordered by $(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3)$,
and reducing to $2$D projects onto the plane spanned by $(u_1,u_2)$ (Figure~\ref{fig:pca-3d}).

\section{A High-Dimensional Example: A 17-Dimensional Dataset}
PCA is often most valuable when $p$ is large and direct visualization is not possible.
To illustrate this, consider a dataset where each observation corresponds to a country and
each feature corresponds to a measured quantity, producing points in $\mathbb{R}^{17}$.
For example, one may treat the weekly per-person consumption of 17 food categories as
a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{17}$. Although we cannot visualize $\mathbb{R}^{17}$ directly, PCA
provides low-dimensional views (e.g., $q=1$ or $q=2$) that reveal dominant contrasts among
countries.

Let $x_k\in\mathbb{R}^{17}$ denote the centered consumption vector for country $k$.
The first PC score is $z_{k1} = u_1^\top x_k$, and the second is $z_{k2}=u_2^\top x_k$.
Countries separated strongly along PC1 exhibit the greatest differences along the dominant
consumption pattern, while PC2 captures the second most important pattern not explained
by PC1.

\section{Summary}
This chapter presented PCA in the general $p$-dimensional setting through the covariance matrix,
its eigendecomposition and the variance-maximization principle, and interpreted it as an
orthogonal projection onto a low-dimensional subspace. The inverse PCA transformation maps the
reduced data back to the feature space, and the reconstruction error, and its mean over many
observations, measures what the dominant directions fail to explain. That error is the PCA
anomaly score used in this thesis.

\section{The CMU CERT Insider Threat Dataset}
\label{sec:cert}
The CERT Insider Threat Test Dataset is a collection of synthetic datasets produced by the CERT
Division of Carnegie Mellon University's Software Engineering Institute, with ExactData, LLC,
under sponsorship from DARPA \cite{glasser2013,certdataset2016}. Each dataset simulates an
organization in which ordinary employees go about their work and a few simulated insiders act out
a scripted malicious scenario. This thesis uses release 4.2, which simulates 1,000 employees whose
recorded activity runs from 2 January 2010 to 17 May 2011. The data contain activity logs
(logons and logoffs, removable-device connections, file operations, emails and web visits),
organizational records (role, business unit, functional unit, department, team, and whether the
employee is an IT administrator), and five personality scores for every employee.

Every log entry is an event $e = (u, \tau, s, \mathbf{a})$: a user $u \in \mathcal{U}$, a time
$\tau$, a source $s$ (logon, device, file, email or web) and a list of attributes $\mathbf{a}$,
with $|\mathcal{U}| = 1{,}000$. The unit of observation is the \emph{user-day} $D = (u, t)$, one
user on one calendar day, whose events are $E_{u,t} = \{ e : e \text{ is an event of } u \text{ on
day } t\}$. Every method in this thesis maps a user-day to an anomaly score $s(D) \in \mathbb{R}$,
and a larger score means a more unusual day.

\section{The Insider Case}
\label{sec:insider-case}
The dataset's answer key names each simulated insider, the scenario that insider follows, and the
malicious events involved \cite{certdataset2016}. Release 4.2 contains 70 insiders in three
scenarios (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}).

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{The three insider scenarios of CERT release 4.2, following the answer key.}
\label{tab:scenarios}
\begin{tabular}{c p{10.8cm} c}
\toprule
scenario & what the insider does & insiders \\
\midrule
1 & Starts working after hours and using a removable drive, which the user had not done before;
uploads data to wikileaks.org and soon leaves. & 30 \\
2 & Browses job websites and seeks work at a competitor; before leaving, copies data with a thumb
drive at a much higher rate than before. & 30 \\
3 & A disgruntled system administrator installs a keylogger on the supervisor's computer, logs in
as the supervisor with the captured credentials and sends an alarming mass email, then leaves. & 10 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The answer key also gives the time of each insider's first and last malicious event. A user-day
is labelled \emph{malicious} if it falls within that window,
\begin{equation}
y(u,t) \;=\;
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if day } t \text{ lies between the first and last malicious event of insider } u, \\
0 & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:day-label}
\end{equation}
and a user is malicious if any of their days is, $y(u) = \max_t y(u,t)$. This labels 1,883
user-days: 1,676 for scenario 2, whose insiders act over weeks, 187 for scenario 1 and 20 for
scenario 3. A stricter label marks only the days on which a malicious event actually occurs; the
feature extraction used in this thesis \cite{le2020analyzing} records 966 such days (85, 861 and
20 by scenario). The language-model experiments use the window labels, and the PCA experiment
uses the stricter labels. User-level results depend only on which users are malicious, so they
are comparable across the two.

Malicious days are rare. At most 1,883 of the 330,295 user-days with computer activity
(Section~\ref{sec:lm-data}) are malicious, a fraction $\pi \le 1{,}883/330{,}295 = 0.0057$. A rule
that calls every day benign would therefore be correct on at least $99.4\%$ of days while
detecting nothing, so this thesis measures detection by ranking. A detector flags every
observation whose score exceeds a threshold, and each threshold catches some fraction of the
malicious cases (the true positive rate) at the cost of flagging some fraction of the benign ones
(the false positive rate). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve plots these two rates
as the threshold moves from strict to lenient---a curve that rises steeply toward the top-left
corner catches malicious cases before it flags many benign ones, while the diagonal is what random
scores would give. The area under the curve (AUC) is the probability that a randomly chosen
malicious case scores higher than a randomly chosen benign one, so 1 means perfect ranking and 0.5
means chance; Section~\ref{sec:lm-auc} defines the user-level version used in this thesis. Malicious examples are also too few and too varied to
learn from, so every detector is \emph{one-class}: it is fit only on benign training data,
$\mathcal{B} = \{ D : y(D) = 0 \}$ restricted to training users, and scores how far a new day
departs from what it learned. The labels are used only for evaluation.

\section[Data Preparation for PCA Reconstruction Analysis]{Data Preparation for PCA\\ Reconstruction Analysis}
\label{sec:pca-data}
The method of \chref{ch:pca} needs each observation as a vector of $p$ numerical features, so the
activity of each observation is summarized into one row $\mathbf{x}_i^\top$ of a matrix
$X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$. Here an observation is a user-day, and its features are the
day-level features of Le et al. \cite{le2020analyzing}: counts of logons, removable-drive
connections, file accesses, emails and web visits, each split by working hours and after hours
and by the kind of computer, file or website involved (for example, visits to job-search,
cloud-storage or leak websites), averages such as file and email sizes, whether the day is a
weekday, and the user's organizational and personality attributes. Release 4.2 gives
$n = 330{,}452$ user-days with 502 features once identifiers and time indices are removed.
Because PCA is sensitive to scale, each column is standardized with the mean and standard
deviation of the training rows. Standardization needs a positive standard deviation, and 193 of
the 502 columns never vary among the training days, so they are removed, leaving $p = 309$
features. Removing the organizational and personality attributes as well (ten of the 309; the
eleventh, the business unit, takes a single value in this release) leaves 299, the PCA counterpart
of the behavior-only score of \chref{ch:lm}.

PCA is fit on the 287,961 days of the 851 training users of Section~\ref{sec:lm-data}, the users
that also train the language model; all of them are benign, so PCA sees no insider and no
held-out benign user. It keeps the first $q$ principal directions $U_q$, with $q$ the smallest
number whose proportion of variance explained \eqref{eq:evr} reaches $95\%$; this gives $q = 71$
directions, which explain $95.1\%$ of the variance ($q = 63$ without the profile attributes). Each
observation is reduced to its scores and mapped back to the feature space with the inverse PCA
transformation \eqref{eq:inverse-pca}, and its anomaly score is its reconstruction error $e_i$
\eqref{eq:recon-error}; the mean squared error \eqref{eq:mse} summarizes a set of observations.
Benign behavior is reconstructed well by the dominant directions of benign variation, so a large
$e_i$ marks an observation that those directions cannot explain.

The median reconstruction error is 6.6 on the days of the 79 held-out benign users and 17.0 on
the 966 malicious days, and $6.4\%$ of the malicious days exceed the $99$th percentile of the
training errors, against $0.5\%$ of the held-out benign days. On the evaluation pool of
Section~\ref{sec:lm-data} (60 insiders and 79 held-out benign users), scoring each user by their
largest error gives a user-level AUC of
$0.75$ (95\% interval $[0.67, 0.83]$), and $0.76$ $[0.67, 0.83]$ without the profile attributes;
with all 70 insiders it is $0.78$ $[0.70, 0.85]$. The threshold matters somewhat: keeping
$80\%$, $90\%$ or $99\%$ of the variance ($q = 36$, $55$ or $94$) gives $0.81$, $0.75$ and $0.78$.
The removed columns matter as well. Among them are visits to leak and hacking websites and logons
to the supervisor's computer, which no training day contains and which occur, in the evaluation
data, almost only on malicious days. Left in their raw units, as some software does for a
column with zero standard deviation, they carry $99.7\%$ of the malicious days' squared error
and raise the user-level AUC to $0.80$ $[0.73, 0.87]$. On the same users, the language model's
full score reaches $0.65$ and its behavior-only score $0.86$ (\chref{ch:lm}).

\section{Data Preparation for Language-Model Analysis}

session has no text. Each user-day $D$ is written as the text

\chapter{Language-Model Surprisal and Its Decomposition}
\label{ch:lm}

\section{Overview and Motivation}
\chref{ch:pca} scored an observation by how poorly a low-dimensional linear model
reconstructs it. This chapter scores a user-day by how poorly a large language model,
fine-tuned only on benign activity, predicts it. Both scores ask the same question: how badly
does a model of normal behavior account for this observation? Two ideas from \chref{ch:pca}
carry over. The fine-tuning changes each weight matrix only by a low-rank correction (Section~\ref{sec:lm-train}),
and \chref{ch:mech} decomposes the model's internal change with a sparse relative of PCA.
Scoring activity by how poorly a model trained on normal activity predicts it follows earlier
work on log and insider-threat data \cite{du2017deeplog,tuor2017deep}.

The new contribution of this chapter is to take the score apart. We show that it splits
exactly into a part describing \emph{who} the user is and a part describing \emph{what} the
user did, and that the first part actively hides malicious users.

\section{Writing a User-Day as Text}
Each \emph{user-day}, one user on one calendar day, is serialized as text. The record below is
a real benign day from CERT release 4.2 (user \texttt{AAF0535}, day 7). The session line is a

    usb_mean_usb_dur=0 n_file=0 file_n-disk1=0 file_n_exef=0 n_email=3

    http_n_leakf=0 http_n_hackf=0
\end{verbatim}
}
\noindent This user had one working-hours session on their usual computer, lasting 489 minutes,
with 3 emails and 29 web requests. The lines fall into two classes. The \textbf{profile lines}
($P$), \texttt{DAY} and \texttt{PSY}, describe \emph{who} the user is: organizational attributes
and Big-Five personality scores, identical on every day of that user apart from the week number.
The \textbf{behavior lines} ($B$), \texttt{SESSIONS} and \texttt{SES}, describe \emph{what the
user did} that day.

The model reads \emph{tokens} $x_1,\dots,x_n$, short pieces of text in which numbers are split
into single digits, so \texttt{dept=15} is the four tokens \texttt{dept}, \texttt{=}, \texttt{1},
\texttt{5}. A token's class is the class of the line it lies on,
\[
c(x_t) \;=\;
\begin{cases}
P & \text{if } x_t \text{ lies on a \texttt{DAY} or \texttt{PSY} line,} \\
B & \text{if } x_t \text{ lies on a \texttt{SESSIONS} or \texttt{SES} line,}
\end{cases}
\]
so the class is decided by where a token sits, not by what it is: the digit \texttt{1} is a
profile token in \texttt{team=11} and a behavior token in \texttt{n\_logon=1}. The record above
has $N = 212$ scored tokens, of which $N_P = 54$ are profile tokens and $N_B = 158$ behavior
tokens; averaged over the malicious days of release 4.2, about 22\% of scored tokens are profile
tokens. Each token's surprisal is counted in the class of the token being predicted, while the
model still reads the whole preceding text as context: the split decides which predictions are
\emph{counted}, not what the model \emph{sees}.

\section{Next-Token Probability and Surprisal}
\label{sec:lm-softmax}
Before seeing token $x_t$, the model assigns a probability to every item of its vocabulary
$\mathcal{V}$ (151,669 tokens). It first condenses the preceding tokens into a \emph{context
vector} $\mathbf{h}_{t-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, $d = 4096$, computed by a stack of 36 layers
\cite{vaswani2017attention}. Each vocabulary item $v$ has a learned vector $\mathbf{w}_v$, and its
score, or \emph{logit}, is the dot product $o_v = \mathbf{w}_v^\top\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$; all scores
at once are $\mathbf{o} = W_{\mathrm{out}}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$. The \emph{softmax} turns scores into
probabilities,
\begin{equation}
p_\theta(x_t = v \mid x_{<t}) \;=\; \frac{e^{o_v}}{\sum_{v' \in \mathcal{V}} e^{o_{v'}}} ,
\label{eq:softmax}
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ denotes the model's parameters. Every probability is positive and they sum to 1;
only differences between scores matter, since $p(a)/p(b) = e^{o_a - o_b}$; and no token is ever
assigned probability zero.

The \emph{surprisal} of the actual token is
\begin{equation}
\ell_t \;=\; -\log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})
\;=\; \mathrm{LSE}(\mathbf{o}) \;-\; o_{x_t},
\qquad \mathrm{LSE}(\mathbf{o}) = \log\sum_{v} e^{o_v},
\label{eq:lse-surprisal}
\end{equation}
where the log-sum-exp satisfies $o_{\max} \le \mathrm{LSE}(\mathbf{o}) \le o_{\max} + \log|\mathcal{V}|$.
Surprisal is therefore how far the actual token's score falls below a soft maximum of all the
scores: a token the benign-trained model scored near the top costs little, and a token it scored
far below the others costs a lot.

\section{Why the Negative Logarithm Measures Surprise}
\label{sec:why-log}
Two natural requirements on a measure of surprise $S(p)$ for an event of probability $p$ leave
only the logarithm, the measure of information introduced by Shannon \cite{shannon1948}. First,
rarer events are more surprising, so $S$ decreases in $p$. Second, the surprises of independent
events add, and since $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A)P(B)$ this means
\begin{equation}
S(pq) \;=\; S(p) + S(q) \qquad \text{for all } p, q \in (0, 1].
\label{eq:surprise-additive}
\end{equation}
Writing $p = e^{-x}$ and $g(x) = S(e^{-x})$ turns \eqref{eq:surprise-additive} into Cauchy's
equation $g(x+y) = g(x) + g(y)$, whose only increasing solutions are $g(x) = c\,x$. Hence
\begin{equation}
S(p) \;=\; -c \ln p, \qquad c > 0,
\label{eq:surprise-log}
\end{equation}
where $c$ only fixes the unit; this thesis uses $c = 1$, the \emph{nat}. A certain event carries
no surprise, an event thought impossible would be infinitely surprising, and every tenfold drop in
probability adds the same $\ln 10$ nats.

For a single observed event, the surprisal is also its \emph{negative log-likelihood}. By the
chain rule of probability, the probability of a day's text is the product of each token's
probability given the tokens before it, so
\begin{equation}
-\log p_\theta(x_2, \dots, x_n \mid x_1)
\;=\; \sum_{t=2}^{n} -\log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})
\;=\; \sum_{t=2}^{n} \ell_t :
\label{eq:lm-chain-rule}
\end{equation}
the total surprisal of a day is the negative log-likelihood of its text. Because days differ in
length (about 200 to 530 tokens), the anomaly score of Section~\ref{sec:lm-score} divides this
total by the number of scored tokens.

\section{Learning From Benign Days Only}
\label{sec:lm-train}
Fine-tuning chooses $\theta$ to minimize the total surprisal of the benign training text,
\begin{equation}
\theta^\star \;=\; \arg\min_{\theta}\; \sum_{D \in \mathcal{B}} \;\sum_{t}\;
-\log p_\theta\bigl(x^{(D)}_t \mid x^{(D)}_{<t}\bigr),
\label{eq:lm-objective}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{B}$ is the set of benign training days and $x^{(D)}$ is the text of day $D$; this
is maximum-likelihood estimation. For the simplest model, one probability $p_j$ for each of $K$
tokens, minimizing $\sum_j N_j(-\log p_j)$ subject to $\sum_j p_j = 1$ with a Lagrange multiplier
gives
\begin{equation}
\hat{p}_j \;=\; \frac{N_j}{N},
\label{eq:lm-mle}
\end{equation}
the benign frequencies: values common among benign users become cheap and rare values expensive,
which is why surprisal under a benign-trained model works as an anomaly score. Fine-tuning performs
the same minimization for a model whose probabilities depend on the whole preceding text, by
gradient descent.

Fine-tuning does not change every weight. It adds a low-rank correction and keeps the original
weights frozen \cite{hu2022lora}: $W' = W + GH$ with $G \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$,
$H \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times d}$ and $r = 16$. For a $4096 \times 4096$ matrix the correction has
$131{,}072$ entries, $0.78\%$ of the $16{,}777{,}216$ in the matrix, and whatever the input
$\mathbf{x}$, the change $GH\mathbf{x}$ lies in the span of the 16 columns of $G$. Just as the
rank-$q$ reconstruction of \chref{ch:pca} keeps only $q$ directions, fine-tuning can change each
adapted matrix's output along at most 16 directions; the parallel is structural only.

\section{The Anomaly Score of a Day and of a User}
\label{sec:lm-score}
Let $\mathcal{T}$ be the set of scored positions of a day (every token except the first, which
has nothing before it) and $N = |\mathcal{T}|$. The anomaly score of the day is its average
token surprisal,
\begin{equation}
\bar{\ell} \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}} \ell_t .
\label{eq:lm-day-score}
\end{equation}
A high $\bar{\ell}$ means that a model trained only on benign days found this day hard to
predict. A user's score is the score of their most unusual day,
\[
\bar{\ell}(u) \;=\; \max_{D \in \mathcal{D}_u} \bar{\ell}(D),
\]
where $\mathcal{D}_u$ is the set of that user's days.

\section{Splitting the Score by Token Class}
\label{sec:lm-decomp}
Divide the scored positions into profile and behavior positions,
$\mathcal{T} = \mathcal{T}_P \cup \mathcal{T}_B$, with $N = N_P + N_B$, class totals $L_P$ and
$L_B$, and class means $\bar{\ell}_P = L_P/N_P$ and $\bar{\ell}_B = L_B/N_B$. Then
\begin{equation}
\bar{\ell}
\;=\; \frac{1}{N}\bigl(L_P + L_B\bigr)
\;=\; \frac{N_P}{N}\,\bar{\ell}_P \;+\; \frac{N_B}{N}\,\bar{\ell}_B :
\label{eq:lm-decomp}
\end{equation}
the day's score is exactly a weighted average of the profile mean and the behavior mean, weighted
by their shares of the tokens. The behavior-only score
\begin{equation}
\bar{\ell}_B \;=\; \frac{L_B}{N_B} \;=\; \frac{L - L_P}{N - N_P}
\label{eq:lm-behavior}
\end{equation}
uses the same model with no retraining; it simply stops counting profile tokens. Subtracting the
profile mean instead does not work, because
\begin{equation}
\bar{\ell} - \bar{\ell}_P \;=\; \frac{N_B}{N}\bigl(\bar{\ell}_B - \bar{\ell}_P\bigr)
\label{eq:lm-naive}
\end{equation}
measures how much harder the behavior was to predict than the profile, not how hard the behavior
was to predict, and it can reverse the order of two days.

Two user-days each have five scored tokens, one profile token and four behavior tokens, so

\section{Measuring Detection: The User-Level AUC}
\label{sec:lm-auc} %%%%% NEW label (2026-09-28), cited from Chapter III
With one malicious user $m$ compared against $n_-$ benign users $u_1,\dots,u_{n_-}$, the
area under the ROC curve for $m$ is the fraction of benign users that $m$ outranks,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AUC}_m \;=\; \frac{1}{n_-}\sum_{j=1}^{n_-}\mathbb{I}\bigl[\bar{\ell}(m) > \bar{\ell}(u_j)\bigr],
\label{eq:auc}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{I}[\cdot]$ is 1 when the condition holds and 0 otherwise (a tie counts as
$\tfrac12$). Averaging over $M$ malicious users gives $\mathrm{AUC} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_m
\mathrm{AUC}_m$. Because every malicious user is compared with the same benign users, this
average equals the pooled AUC over all $M \times n_-$ malicious--benign pairs:
\[
\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}\frac{1}{n_-}\sum_{j}\mathbb{I}[\cdot]
\;=\; \frac{1}{M\,n_-}\sum_{m}\sum_{j}\mathbb{I}[\cdot].
\]
A value of 0.5 is chance, 1 is perfect, and a value below 0.5 means malicious users are
systematically ranked \emph{below} benign ones.

For example, suppose four benign users have full scores $(0.93,\;0.70,\;0.85,\;0.60)$ and a
malicious user has full score $0.77$. The malicious user outranks $0.70$ and $0.60$, so
$\mathrm{AUC}_m = 2/4 = 0.50$. If the behavior-only scores are $(0.41,\;0.55,\;0.70,\;0.48)$
for the benign users and $0.93$ for the malicious user, it outranks all four, and
$\mathrm{AUC}_m = 4/4 = 1.00$.

\label{sec:lm-finding}
Table~\ref{tab:lm-views} scores the same 60 malicious and 79 benign users of CERT release 4.2

\begin{table}[H]
\centering

training) for scores built from different token classes. Intervals are 95\% bootstrap

\toprule
score & tokens counted & user AUC & 95\% interval \\

full score $\bar{\ell}$ & all & 0.65 & [0.58, 0.72] \\

Scoring behavior tokens alone raises the user-level AUC by $+0.21$, with a 95\% interval of
$[+0.16,\;+0.27]$. Compared one malicious user at a time, 51 of the 60 malicious users are
ranked higher among the benign users, 3 are unchanged, and 6 are ranked lower.

Three observations explain the result.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The profile tokens are not neutral noise.} On their own they rank malicious users
below benign ones (0.47), and the organization line alone does so strongly (0.22). In this
benchmark, malicious users tend to have common profiles, so their profile tokens are cheap and
pull their full scores down: exactly Day A of the worked example.
\item \textbf{Surprisal measures typicality, not suspicion.} Every evaluated user is new to the
model, so a profile's surprisal reflects how common that kind of employee was among benign
training users. A rare but innocent job title is surprising; a malicious user with an ordinary
job title is not.
\item \textbf{The fix requires no new model.} The decomposition \eqref{eq:lm-decomp} is
exact, so the behavior-only score is obtained from the same model outputs by not counting
profile tokens. It removes the profile's direct contribution to the score, not its influence:
the profile is still in the context, so every behavior-token prediction is still conditioned on
it.
\end{itemize}
The same check applies to any detector whose input mixes who a user is with what they did.

The user-level AUC measures how well users are ranked. It does not show that the score
pinpoints the specific malicious day, and it should not be read that way.

\chapter{Interpretable Dimensionality Reduction of the Fine-Tuned Model}
\label{ch:mech}

\section{Overview and Motivation}
\chref{ch:lm} examined the score from the outside: which tokens its surprisal comes
from. \emph{Mechanistic interpretability} asks what the model computes internally
\cite{bricken2023monosemanticity,cunningham2023sparse}. This chapter answers that question with
a form of dimensionality reduction designed for interpretation. PCA (\chref{ch:pca}) reduces
data to a few dense directions, each of which mixes many original features. A \emph{sparse
autoencoder} instead describes each observation with a few directions drawn from a much larger
dictionary; because only a few are active at once, each direction, or \emph{feature}, tends to
mean one recognizable thing. We apply it to the change that benign fine-tuning made to the
model's internal representations, and test two things about the features that best separate
malicious from benign days: where they activate, and whether editing them changes the anomaly
score.

\section{Hidden States and the Adaptation Difference}
The model processes text through a stack of 36 layers. After each layer, each token $t$ is
represented by a \emph{hidden state} $\mathbf{h}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ with $d = 4096$. Running
the same text through the model before fine-tuning (the \emph{base} model) and after it (the
\emph{adapted} model) gives two hidden states for every token. Their difference,
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\delta}_t \;=\; \mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{ada}}_t - \mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{base}}_t
\;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{d},
\label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
is exactly what benign fine-tuning added to the representation of token $t$. We study the
hidden states after layer 26.

\section{A Sparse Dictionary for the Difference}
Recall the PCA reconstruction of \chref{ch:pca}: an observation is encoded by
$\mathbf{z} = U_q^\top \mathbf{x}$ using $q < p$ orthonormal directions, decoded by
$\widehat{\mathbf{x}} = U_q \mathbf{z}$, and the directions minimize the reconstruction error
\eqref{eq:recon-error}. A \emph{sparse autoencoder} (SAE) keeps the reconstruction objective but
changes two things: it uses \emph{more} directions than dimensions ($m > d$), and it lets only a
few of them be active for any one token \cite{gao2024scaling}.

To encode, compute a score for each of the $m$ dictionary directions and keep only the $k$
largest:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}_t = W_{\mathrm{enc}}(\boldsymbol{\delta}_t - \mathbf{b}) \in \mathbb{R}^{m},
\qquad
\mathbf{z}_t = \mathrm{TopK}_k(\mathbf{a}_t),
\label{eq:sae-encode}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{TopK}_k$ keeps the $k$ largest entries and sets the rest to zero.

The difference is then decoded from the active directions:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_t = W_{\mathrm{dec}}\,\mathbf{z}_t + \mathbf{b}
= \sum_{f:\, z_{f,t}\neq 0} z_{f,t}\,\mathbf{w}_f + \mathbf{b},
\label{eq:sae-decode}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{w}_f$ is column $f$ of $W_{\mathrm{dec}}$, the direction of \emph{feature} $f$.

Training chooses $W_{\mathrm{enc}}$, $W_{\mathrm{dec}}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ to minimize
$\sum_t \|\boldsymbol{\delta}_t - \widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_t\|_2^2$, the same squared
reconstruction error as PCA. No labels are used. (Each coordinate of $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ is
standardized before encoding and restored after decoding; we suppress this in the notation.)

\noindent With $m = 8{,}192$ directions but only $k = 4$ active per token, each token's
$\boldsymbol{\delta}_t$ is explained by four features. Because so few are active at once, each
feature tends to specialize in one recognizable pattern. Our dictionary is trained on
2,000,000 token-level differences.

\section{A Numerical Example of Sparse Coding}
Take $d = 2$, a dictionary of $m = 3$ unit directions $\mathbf{w}_1 = (1,\;0)$,
$\mathbf{w}_2 = (0,\;1)$ and $\mathbf{w}_3 = (0.6,\;0.8)$, one active feature ($k = 1$),
$\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$ and $W_{\mathrm{enc}} = W_{\mathrm{dec}}^\top$. For
$\boldsymbol{\delta} = (1.2,\;1.6)$ the scores are $\mathbf{a} = (1.2,\;1.6,\;2.0)$, so feature 3
is kept and $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}} = 2.0\,\mathbf{w}_3 = (1.2,\;1.6)$ with zero error.
With only the two axes available, the best single direction would leave an error of
$1.2^2 = 1.44$. A different token, $\boldsymbol{\delta}' = (1.0,\;0.1)$, is described by feature 1
with error $0.01$: each token uses the few features that fit it best, which is \emph{sparse
coding}.

\section{Choosing the Features to Test}
For each feature $f$, its \emph{gap} compares how strongly it fires on malicious and benign
days,
\begin{equation}
g_f \;=\; \bar{z}_f^{(+)} - \bar{z}_f^{(-)},
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{z}_f^{(+)}$ and $\bar{z}_f^{(-)}$ are the average activations of feature $f$ over
the tokens of malicious days and of benign days. The \emph{selected set} $\mathcal{F}$ is the
five features with the largest gaps. The \emph{control set} $\mathcal{C}$ is the five features with
the gaps closest to zero among those active on at least 0.2\% of tokens. The threshold keeps
rarely firing features out of the control set, but it does not match the two sets' activity: in
the held-out test below, the control features were active on 0.36\% of tokens on average and the
selected features on 0.18\%. The control answers the question: does editing \emph{any} active
features change the score, or only the selected ones?

\section{Where Features Fire: Token-Class Attribution}
For a feature $f$ and a token class $c$, the share of the feature's total activation that
lands on class-$c$ tokens is
\begin{equation}
\rho_f(c) \;=\; \frac{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}_c} z_{f,t}}{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} z_{f,t}},
\label{eq:attribution}
\end{equation}
and its \emph{enrichment} compares that share with the class's share of the tokens,
\[
E_f(c) \;=\; \frac{\rho_f(c)}{N_c / N}.
\]
An enrichment of 1 means the feature fires on class $c$ exactly as often as chance would
suggest; larger values mean it prefers class $c$, up to the maximum $1/(N_c/N)$ reached when all
of a feature's activation lands on that class.

\section{Causal Test: Editing Features and Re-Scoring}
Attribution shows where a feature fires, not whether it matters. The causal test edits the
features and measures the change in the anomaly score. Take a malicious \emph{receiver} day.
A \emph{donor} day comes from another user in the same department, and it is one of two kinds:
a benign day of a colleague, or a malicious day of another malicious user. Comparing the two
kinds of donor asks whether the benign colleague's values in particular make the receiver look
normal, rather than any edit of the same features.

The test has six steps.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Encode each receiver token with \eqref{eq:sae-encode}.
\item On every token where at least one selected feature is active, replace the activation of
\emph{every} selected feature, including features that were zero at that token, by
$z'_{f,t} = (1-\gamma)\,z_{f,t} + \gamma\,\bar{z}^{\,\mathrm{donor}}_f$ for $f \in \mathcal{F}$,
with patch strength $\gamma \in \{0.25,\;0.5,\;0.75,\;1\}$; all other features are unchanged.
Here $\bar{z}^{\,\mathrm{donor}}_f$ is the average activation of feature $f$ over the donor day's
tokens on which at least one selected feature is active.
\item Decode the edited code, $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}'_t = W_{\mathrm{dec}}\mathbf{z}'_t +
\mathbf{b}$, and replace the adapted hidden state at layer 26 by
$\mathbf{h}'_t = \mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{ada}}_t + (\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}'_t -
\boldsymbol{\delta}_t) = \mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{base}}_t + \widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}'_t$. If any
token of the day has an active selected feature, this replacement is made at every token of the
day: tokens without one receive their unedited reconstruction, and a day with no active selected
feature is left unchanged. The reconstruction is not exact, so it can move the score even without
an edit; step 6 is built so that this movement cancels.
\item Run the remaining layers from $\mathbf{h}'$ and recompute the day's score $\bar{\ell}'$; the
change $\delta = \bar{\ell}' - \bar{\ell}$ is negative when the edit makes the day look less
anomalous.
\item For each receiver day, try up to 16 candidate donor days of each kind at all four patch
strengths, and keep the most negative change for each kind, giving
$\delta_{\mathcal{F},\mathrm{ben}}$ and $\delta_{\mathcal{F},\mathrm{mal}}$; the control set gives
$\delta_{\mathcal{C},\mathrm{ben}}$ and $\delta_{\mathcal{C},\mathrm{mal}}$.
\item Compare donors, then feature sets, through the donor advantages
$a_{\mathcal{F}} = \delta_{\mathcal{F},\mathrm{mal}} - \delta_{\mathcal{F},\mathrm{ben}}$ and
$a_{\mathcal{C}} = \delta_{\mathcal{C},\mathrm{mal}} - \delta_{\mathcal{C},\mathrm{ben}}$.
\end{enumerate}
The effect of the selected features is the average, over receiver days that have all four
values, of
\begin{equation}
\tau \;=\; a_{\mathcal{F}} - a_{\mathcal{C}}
\;=\; \bigl(\delta_{\mathcal{F},\mathrm{mal}} - \delta_{\mathcal{F},\mathrm{ben}}\bigr)
- \bigl(\delta_{\mathcal{C},\mathrm{mal}} - \delta_{\mathcal{C},\mathrm{ben}}\bigr).
\label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
The unedited score cancels, and so, within one feature set, does the part of the reconstruction's
effect that simply adds to the edit's; the layers after the edit are nonlinear, so the rest does
not cancel. A positive $\tau$ means that moving the selected features toward a benign colleague,
rather than toward another malicious user, lowers the score by more than the same comparison does
for the control features. A simpler comparison, $\delta_{\mathcal{C},\mathrm{ben}} -
\delta_{\mathcal{F},\mathrm{ben}}$, uses benign donors only. It is a different quantity: it mixes
the effect of moving toward benign values with any effect of editing the selected features at
all, which $\tau$ removes, and the interval reported below belongs to $\tau$ only.

\section{Core Mechanistic Finding}
\label{sec:mech-finding}

Table~\ref{tab:attribution} gives the attribution \eqref{eq:attribution} of the five features
selected on all 60 malicious users of CERT release 4.2.

3673 & 1.000 & 0.000 & 0.000 & 1.335 on \texttt{SES} \\

Four of the five features put at least 99.9\% of their activation on session tokens. Their
enrichment of 1.334 to 1.335 is the maximum possible, since $1/0.7492 = 1.335$: essentially all
of their activation lands on the session lines. The strongest activations of one of them
(3673) all fall on the \texttt{\_dur} token that names the session-duration fields. The fifth
feature (2302) fires on the organization line instead.

This table answers one question only: \emph{where} each feature activates. It does not say what
information a feature represents; a feature that activates on session tokens could still carry
information about who the user is, such as the session lengths typical of a department. Nor does
it say which predictions change when the feature is edited. Those are separate questions.

For the causal test, the 60 malicious users were split in half. Features were re-selected using
only the 30 \emph{discovery} users; the re-selected set shares four of the five features above.
The test was then run on the other 30 malicious users, whom feature selection never saw. With
donors from the same department, \eqref{eq:tau} gave
\[
\tau \;=\; 0.00076 \text{ nats}, \qquad 95\%\text{ interval } [0.00035,\;0.00121],
\]
over the 605 held-out malicious days with all four values, and $\tau$ was positive for 21 of 29
held-out users. The interval resamples users. In the same data the donor advantage was
$a_{\mathcal{F}} = 0.00063$ for the selected features and $a_{\mathcal{C}} = -0.00012$ for the
controls. The benign-only comparison is $0.0028$ here; it has no interval of its own.
The estimate is positive in all four organizational groupings tested (department, department
and role, team, role: 0.00034 to 0.00076 nats); the department grouping is the one whose
interval excludes zero.

A later rescoring of the same 638 held-out malicious days repeated every one of these edits
exactly and recorded the change in loss separately for profile tokens and behavior tokens. It
reproduces the value above to six decimals when step 5 keeps the best candidate. When the
candidates are averaged instead of the best one being kept, at full strength ($\gamma = 1$), the
same comparison is negative in every grouping: $-0.0028$ nats for department on the average over
users, with 95\% interval $[-0.0045, -0.0012]$, and intervals that include zero for the other
three. Averaging over all four strengths also gives negative values. The positive $\tau$ above
therefore describes the best of the searched edits. It does not show that a typical edit toward a
benign colleague makes a malicious day look normal.

Split by token class, with benign donors at full strength and averaged over donors, the selected
edits raise the loss on behavior tokens by $0.016$ to $0.018$ nats per token more than the control
edits do, in every grouping, with every interval excluding zero. At strengths $0.25$ and $0.5$ the
difference is slightly negative. The difference on profile tokens is uncertain (every interval
includes zero), and the direct comparison between the two classes, which was declared as the
main quantity before the rescoring, is inconclusive. So the result shows neither that the edits
leave profile predictions alone nor that the two effects differ. The profile lines come before
the session lines, and each token is predicted only from earlier tokens, so an edit on a session
token can change behavior predictions but not profile predictions. The edit is joint: at the
organization-line tokens where feature 2302 fires, every selected feature is written, so any
profile effect cannot be credited to feature 2302 alone.

Three things are established. The features that most distinguish malicious days activate almost
only on session tokens. On users held out from feature selection, the best of the searched edits
favors a benign donor more for the selected features than for the controls, by a small margin,
while the average edit does not. And the joint edit of the selected features raises behavior-token
loss more than the control edit at the two larger strengths. Several things are not established:
what information the features represent; whether the behavior effect exceeds what an equally
large change of the hidden state at the same tokens would cause, since the selected edits are
several times larger than the control edits; whether it is specific to malicious days, since every
edited day was malicious; whether the edits change profile predictions; and whether the profile
shortcut of \chref{ch:lm} arises mainly from averaging surprisal over every token, since the
score decomposition shows that the shortcut is removable at scoring time but not where it arises
inside the model.

The best-candidate effect is small in absolute terms ($0.00076$ nats against day scores near
1), and it is about half the size of the estimate obtained before the held-out split.

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item The dictionary's layer, size and sparsity (layer 26, $m = 8{,}192$, $k = 4$) were chosen
among twelve candidate settings using runs that included all 60 malicious users. The
features were re-selected on held-out users, but this setting was not.
\item All results come from a single fine-tuned model, and their stability across training
seeds is untested. A companion-dataset result once cited here as a seed reversal selected its
features from an undefined ranking and has been withdrawn.
\item Step 5 of the test keeps the best of many candidates. That favours whichever set's candidate scores vary
more, and there can be fewer malicious than benign donors, so $\tau$ can be positive without a
donor-specific effect. Averaged over all candidates, the comparison is negative.
\item The controls pass an activity threshold but are not activity-matched to the selected
features, and the selected edits change the hidden state several times more than the control
edits.
\item Every edit replaces the hidden-state change with the dictionary's reconstruction at every
token of the day before editing, and edits all selected features together. A version that keeps
the original hidden state and adds only the edit, $\mathbf{h}' = \mathbf{h} + W_{\mathrm{dec}}
(\mathbf{z}' - \mathbf{z})$, has not been used for the reported results.
\item One benchmark release was used, CERT release 4.2. It is synthetic, and it was evaluated
many times during development.
\item The user-level AUC ranks users; it does not show that the model identifies the specific
malicious day.
\end{itemize}
