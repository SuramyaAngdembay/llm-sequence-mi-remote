\begin{abstract}
Language models adapted to benign activity logs can rank insider-threat days
highly, but a ranking score does not reveal what such a detector has learned:
unusual behavior, profile novelty, and exposure during adaptation can all raise
it. In this work, we audit Qwen3-8B adapters trained on non-insider employee
logs from the CERT r4.2 and r6.2 insider-threat benchmarks, separating score
construction, evaluation population, input sensitivity, and internal
intervention effects. A calendar-label audit first corrects the evaluated
populations to \rv{r42.my_positive_days} positive days from
\rv{r42.my_positive_users} insiders (r4.2) and \rv{r62.my_positive_days} days
from \rv{r62.my_positive_users} insiders (r6.2); the adapters' training
membership is unaffected. Against negatives from the general user population,
fold day ROC-AUC is \rv{r42.all_users.day_roc} on r4.2 and
\rv{r62.all_users.day_roc} on r6.2; against benign users unseen during
adaptation, it falls to \rv{r42.unseen_users.day_roc} and
\rv{r62.unseen_users.day_roc}. Because the score averages over every target
token, profile fields included, we decompose it with the model and input held
fixed: averaging only behavior targets raises r4.2 fold day ROC-AUC from
\rv{c32.full.day_roc} to \rv{c32.behavior_only.day_roc}, a score mitigation
rather than evidence that the model ignores profiles. Inside the model,
sparse-autoencoder edits of adapter-induced representation changes show
predictive dependence without attack specificity: complete r4.2 ablation
raises behavior loss but has a negative paired incident-window contrast, and
fresh r6.2 behavioral-feature removal is not distinguished from norm-matched
controls. We report each verified result with its scope and identify the
repairs required before earlier causal claims can be reinstated.
\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}
High ranking quality does not identify which information an anomaly detector
uses. In employee activity logs, unusual behavior, profile novelty, and training
exposure can all influence a score. We ask how these influences can be separated
using frozen-model score decompositions, input manipulations, and internal
interventions. This recovery edition distinguishes established measurements
from claims still awaiting corrected experiments. Its contribution is an
explicit audit and evidence record, not a detector leaderboard or a new
mathematical theorem.
